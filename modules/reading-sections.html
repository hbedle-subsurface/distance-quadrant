Module 13  ·  The DQ trace

Reading a DQ section

Every attribute so far has been shown as a curve. On a real project they arrive as sections, and a DQ section is not colored like a seismic section — deliberately. The display conventions are the subject here: what each color bar is doing, why it is built that way, and what you would misread if it were built the ordinary way.

What the data does to it

Work through the three steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references are there when you want them.

1The seismic convention

Symmetric, because amplitude is symmetric

A seismic section is colored red through white to blue, with the two ends the same distance from zero. That symmetry is not decoration: amplitude is a signed quantity whose two signs mean equally important and opposite things, so a color bar that treated them differently would be lying about the data. The limits are set by scanning the volume and rounding up, and white sits at zero.

Over the color goes a wiggle, scaled up so the deflections read — about twice the trace spacing is a usual starting point — and horizontal dotted reference lines every ten, twenty, twenty-five, fifty or a hundred milliseconds depending on how much time is on the page.

Note what the reader has to do here: the sand is a pair of colored bands, and telling where it starts and stops means deciding which band belongs to the top and which to the base. That is the ordinary work of seismic interpretation, and it is what the next display is trying to avoid.

2The DQ convention

One-sided, because the interest is one-sided

A DQ section is colored differently, and the difference is deliberate. The color bar is one-sided: the full range of increments is spent on zero to the maximum, and everything negative gets two increments and no more. On a soft-sand play the sands are the positive samples, and the shales are mostly negative, so the bar spends its resolution where the interest is and compresses the rest.

The low and negative end is light gray rather than white. White washes out against the page and makes the background look like missing data; gray reads as background. The overlay wiggle is scaled up less than on a seismic section, about one and a half times, because the DQ trace is blocked rather than oscillatory and does not need as much exaggeration to be legible.

The sand now reads as a single colored body rather than as two bands with a gap. That is the practical payoff of everything modules 02 to 07 did: the picking, the numbering and the phase filters exist to make each loop carry one value, and a one-sided color bar is how you display something that is one value per loop rather than an oscillation.

3The angle convention

Two-sided, banded, and deliberately not smooth

θpx gets a third treatment: two-sided from −90 to +90, but with bolder colors on the positive side for the same reason the DQ bar is one-sided, and divided into ten-degree bands with the boundaries kept sharp rather than smoothly graded.

The sharp boundaries are the unusual choice. Every instinct in modern display design says to interpolate, and here it would be wrong: a feature confined to one ten-degree band — the papers give a flat spot as the example — disappears into a smooth ramp and survives a banded one. The two sections are meant to be read as a pair, so they share a sign convention and a geometry, and only the coloring differs.

?Display is not decoration

Three bars, three arguments

Each of these three color bars encodes a claim about what its attribute is for. The seismic bar is symmetric because amplitude's two signs matter equally. The DQ bar is one-sided because the attribute is a distance and the interest is at one end. The angle bar is banded because the useful features are narrow and a smooth ramp would swallow them.

Get any of them wrong and you have not corrupted the data, you have hidden part of it. A symmetric bar on a DQ section spends half its resolution on shale. A smooth ramp on an angle section loses a flat spot. A linear count axis on a DQ histogram buries the tail under the background, which is why the published histograms use a logarithmic one — in a clastic section roughly four samples in five are shale sitting near zero.

Which is the argument for treating display conventions as part of the method rather than as a matter of taste. They are documented alongside the algorithm in the DQ papers for the same reason the sample rate is: get them wrong and the output no longer shows you what it computed.

Exercises

  1. Compare the two sections. Look at step 1 and step 2 with the same model.
    AnswerOn the seismic section the sand is a pair of bands with a gap between them, and locating it means deciding which band is the top and which the base. On the DQ section it is one body. Nothing has been added — the DQ trace is computed from the same two partial stacks — but the display is showing one value per loop instead of an oscillation, which is what the picking and numbering of modules 02 and 04 were for.
  2. Count the increments. On step 2, read how the color bar is divided.
    AnswerThe full set above zero and only two below. That is the published specification, and the reasoning is that on a soft-sand play the sands are the positive samples and the shales are mostly negative. A symmetric bar would spend half its resolution distinguishing shades of background from one another. This is a display choice that assumes a play type, and it would need rethinking on a hard-sand or carbonate project.
  3. Change the fluid and watch the angle section. On step 3, switch between brine and gas.
    AnswerThe colors inside the sand shift, and the range at the sand top changes with them. Be careful reading more into this line than it can carry: it varies bed thickness across the traces, so its thin end is in the tuning regime and its weakest traces are near a crossover where module 07 showed the angle to be unstable. What the banding does is make a change of a few tens of degrees visible at a glance instead of hiding it in a smooth ramp. Whether the angle is responding to fluid rather than to geometry is module 11's question, answered there on a model built for it.
  4. Turn off the overlay wiggle. Untick the box in the panel.
    AnswerThe color alone is easier to read for extent and harder to read for detail. The papers keep both because they answer different questions: the color shows you where a body is, the wiggle shows you what the trace is doing sample by sample. The scaling differs too — about twice for a seismic wiggle, about one and a half times for a DQ wiggle, because a blocked trace needs less exaggeration.
  5. Add noise and see which display degrades first. Take the noise to 30%.
    AnswerThe angle section goes first, because banded color has no tolerance: a sample that moves a few degrees crosses a boundary and changes color completely, where the same movement on a smooth bar would be invisible. Sharp bands make small real features visible and small spurious ones equally visible. That is a trade the convention accepts deliberately, and it is a reason to look at the DQ section and the angle section together rather than either alone.
  6. Look along the line rather than at one trace. The sand thickens from left to right, so the section is a wedge.
    AnswerThe DQ body brightens toward the thin end, which is module 11's tuning seen as a picture rather than as a curve. A reader looking only at the DQ section would call the thin part the better rock. That is the habit worth taking away: a section shows you where something is, and it will happily show you a tuning effect as though it were geology.

The main points

  • Seismic sections use a symmetric red-white-blue bar, limits scanned from the data and rounded up, because amplitude's two signs matter equally.
  • DQ sections use a one-sided bar: full increments from zero to the maximum, two increments for everything negative.
  • Low and negative values are light gray, not white, so the background reads as background rather than as missing data.
  • θpx sections are two-sided from −90 to +90, bolder on the positive side, in ten-degree bands.
  • The band boundaries are kept sharp on purpose, so that a feature confined to one band survives instead of dissolving into a ramp.
  • Overlay wiggles are scaled about twice for seismic and one and a half times for DQ, because a blocked trace needs less exaggeration.
  • DQ histograms need a logarithmic count axis, because most samples in a clastic section are background near zero.
  • The DQ and angle sections are meant to be read as a pair, sharing a sign convention and a geometry so that only the coloring differs.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The section is built by running the whole workflow of modules 02 through 10 at each of twenty-one trace positions, with the sand thickening linearly across the line from about a third of the setting in the panel to about twice it. Everything else is held constant, so the only thing varying along the line is bed thickness.

The display conventions are taken from the DQ display specification and reproduced as closely as this format allows: the symmetric seismic bar with limits scanned and rounded, the one-sided DQ bar with two increments below zero and light gray rather than white at the low end, the two-sided angle bar in ten-degree steps with sharp boundaries and bolder colors on the positive side, and the overlay wiggle scalars. The specification also describes color interpolation between samples in six directions on a 3D cube, which this page does not attempt; here the color is drawn as flat cells one sample tall.

The remark that roughly four samples in five are shale comes from the same specification and describes a field clastic section, not this model, where the proportion depends on how thick you make the sand. It is quoted because it is the reason for the logarithmic histogram axis, which module 12 uses.

What this module does not do. It does not reproduce the published sections, which come from ray-traced synthetics over pseudo-well catalogs and from field data. It shows what the conventions are and what each one is protecting, on a model simple enough that you can see the convention rather than the geology.
