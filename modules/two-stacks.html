Module 01  ·  Part 1: Why a new trace

Two stacks, and what AVO reads from them

The DQ workflow starts from two partial stacks cut from one angle gather: a near stack and a far stack. Between them they carry the intercept and the gradient of conventional AVO.

Three steps in order, then the exercises.

1Two windows on a gather

An angle gather holds one trace per incidence angle at a single CDP. After NMO correction the top and base of the sand arrive at the same two-way time on every trace, and only the amplitude changes with angle.

The near stack N is the average of the traces from 0 to 10°. The far stack F is the average of a ten-degree window further out, 20 to 30° in the DQ papers. Subtracting one from the other gives a third trace, F − N, which records how much the reflection changed between the two windows.

The near window stays at 0–10°. The far window is ten degrees wide.

2Intercept and gradient

At a single boundary the reflection coefficient plotted against sin²θ is close to a straight line (Shuey, 1985): R(θ) = A + B sin²θ. A is where the line meets zero angle. B, the gradient, is how steeply the line rises or falls.

The average of a straight line over a window is the line’s value at the window’s mean sin²θ. That mean is 0.0101 for 0–10° and 0.1802 for 20–30°. So N = A + 0.0101 B, which is the intercept to within a hundredth of the gradient, and F − N = 0.1701 B, the gradient scaled down by the separation of the two windows.

The right-hand plot is a crossplot of N against F − N, the form the DQ papers use. Both axes share one fixed scale, so the 0.17 factor shows as a compressed vertical spread.

Changing the pore fluid moves the sand-top point. Gas lowers the sand’s P-wave velocity strongly and its density slightly, so its impedance drops and A becomes more negative. Gas lowers VP much more than VS, so VP/VS falls and B generally becomes more negative as well.

3Where the reading holds

The equation in step 2 describes one boundary and no wavelet, the two-layer model. A seismic trace is the reflectivity convolved with a wavelet, so each sample mixes the top and base reflections and their sidelobes.

At a peak or trough one boundary dominates, and the sample lands near the two-layer answer on the crossplot. Between the extrema the mixture changes from sample to sample, and the points form a path through the origin. The two-layer reading is cleanest at the extrema: two samples in each waveform cycle, out of 25 at 40 Hz and a 1 ms sample rate.

The DQ workflow is designed to convert every sample to the equivalent of a peak or trough, so that the two-layer reading can be applied to all of them (Bedle et al., 2025a). The papers note where this works less well: in beds thinner than about a quarter wavelength, and where the lithology changes within one waveform loop. Modules 02 to 05 build the conversion one operation at a time.

Exercises

  1. How much of the gradient reaches F − N? With the default gas sand, how does the F − N readout on step 1 compare with the gradient B on step 2?
    AnswerF − N at the sand top is −0.042, and B is −0.247. The ratio is the 0.1701 from step 2. The far-minus-near trace is the gradient scaled by how far apart the two windows sit in sin²θ.
  2. What does the far window position do to that ratio? How does the ratio on step 2 change as the far window (its slider is on step 1) moves from 12–22° out to 20–30°?
    AnswerThe ratio rises from 0.0775 to 0.1701. A wider separation between the windows gives a larger F − N for the same gradient. Farther angles are also noisier and more affected by residual moveout, and the two-term equation drifts past about 30°, which is why the papers stop there.
  3. Can the near stack alone identify gas? Under soft shale, compare a gas sand at 28% porosity with a brine sand at 36%. What do N and F − N read at the sand top?
    AnswerThe near stack reads almost the same for both, −0.041 for the gas sand and −0.042 for the brine sand. Higher porosity and gas both lower the sand’s impedance, so both lower the intercept. F − N differs: −0.042 with gas and −0.013 with brine, because gas lowers VP/VS much more than extra porosity does. In this model the gradient separates the two; a near stack on its own would not.
  4. Does the trough still give the two-layer answer when the sand is thin? With the step 3 cursor on the trough at the sand top, what happens to the distance readout as the sand thins from 30 ms to 10 ms?
    AnswerAt 30 ms the trough sits 0% from the two-layer answer. At 10 ms the top and base reflections overlap, the trough moves, and it lands 45% away. This is tuning: even the extrema stop being single-boundary readings once the bed is thin.
  5. Does one crossplot position mean one kind of sand? On the step 2 crossplot, compare a brine sand at 36% porosity under soft shale with a gas sand at 20% porosity under hard shale.
    AnswerThe two sand tops plot close together, at about (−0.042, −0.013) and (−0.056, −0.018). One is wet and one holds gas. The crossplot records the contrast between the sand and the rock above it, so a change in the overlying rock can move the point as far as a change in fluid. Reading the fluid from the point requires some independent knowledge of the rock above the sand, from a well or from the regional geology.
  6. What does a zero crossing contribute? Where does the crossplot point sit when the step 3 cursor is halfway between the trough and the peak?
    AnswerThe point sits close to the origin of the crossplot. The sample is real data from inside the sand, yet both N and F − N are near zero there, so a conventional AVO reading has nothing to work with. These are the samples the DQ workflow is built to recover.

The main points

  • The DQ workflow runs on two traces: a near stack over 0–10° and a far stack over 20–30°, from one CDP of NMO-corrected, relative-amplitude gathers.
  • N ≈ A. The near stack equals the intercept plus 0.0101 times the gradient.
  • F − N = 0.1701 B. The intercept cancels exactly and the gradient remains, scaled by the separation of the windows.
  • The two-layer reading holds at peaks and troughs, where one boundary dominates the sample. Between them each sample mixes boundaries.
  • Two samples per cycle. At 40 Hz and 1 ms a waveform cycle holds 25 samples, and the two-layer reading is cleanest at two of them.
  • A crossplot position has more than one explanation. Porosity, pore fluid and the rock above the sand all move the sand-top point, and different combinations can land in the same place.
  • Thin beds move even the extrema away from the two-layer answer, through tuning.
Method, assumptions, and references

The rock model is the one used on every page of the site. The rock above and below the sand is one of three fixed lithologies: soft shale, hard shale or tight sandstone. The brine sand is built from porosity with Wyllie’s time average for VP, a linear mixing law for density, and the VP/VS trend of Bedle et al. (2025a, eq. 7). Oil and gas cases follow from Gassmann fluid substitution. Intercept and gradient at each boundary come from the Shuey (1985) two-term approximation.

The gather has 41 traces at one-degree spacing from 0 to 40°. Each trace is the two reflection coefficients at that angle convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet, sampled at 1 ms. A partial stack is the unweighted mean of the traces inside its window. The mean of sin²θ over each window is computed numerically.

Left out here: random noise, residual moveout, NMO stretch, transmission loss and mode conversion. The DQ papers also band-pass filter both stacks where the signal-to-noise ratio calls for it, apply a time-variant scalar to the far stack so its peak amplitudes agree with AVO theory, and sinc interpolate both to 1 ms.

Fitting R = A + B sin²θ through every angle at every sample is the alternative to two window averages. On a perfectly flat, noisy gather the fit carries less noise. With a few milliseconds of residual moveout it carries more bias, because it compares the same sample index at 0° and 40°, which are no longer the same part of the waveform. The near stack spans only a fraction of a millisecond of that moveout, and the DQ workflow picks its layer boundaries from the near stack.

The crossplots use fixed axes of ±0.20, so the same sand always plots at the same size. The stronger contrasts, mostly tight sandstone over a porous sand, fall outside; in step 2 they are drawn as open triangles at the edge, and in step 3 the samples are pinned to the edge.
