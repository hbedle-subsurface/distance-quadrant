Module 10  ·  The DQ trace

Theta PX

The DQ distance says how far a sample sits from the origin. This is the other half of the same vector: which direction it points, folded into a single range and given a sign. It is the attribute that separates fluids in places where the distance cannot, and it fails in a specific, predictable way that is worth knowing before you trust it.

What the data does to it

Work through the three steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references are there when you want them.

1Folding the angle

Four sectors, one range

Take the coordinates module 09 produced and measure the bearing of each sample from the origin. That bearing runs from 0 to 360°, and the four sectors of the plot carry overlapping information: a direction and its opposite describe the same contrast seen from opposite ends. So the bearing is folded into 0 to 90°, by a rule that depends on which sector the sample is in.

Each sector folds onto the same quarter. A point at 170° and a point at 10° both come out at 10°; the fold has thrown away which sector they were in, and step 2 puts back the part of that which mattered.

The fold is a measurement of how far the sample sits from the background trend in direction rather than in distance. Zero means the sample lies along the rotated baseline — the background itself. Ninety means it sits at right angles to it, which is as far from ordinary as the angle can report.

2Adding the sign

Which side of the background the sample sits on

The folded angle is a magnitude between 0 and 90° and cannot say which way the departure runs. The sign of the DQ trace supplies that: whatever polarity module 07 gave the distance at a sample, θpx at that sample takes the same one. The result runs from −90 to +90°, and the two halves are read differently.

This is also why the two attributes are designed to be displayed together. They share a sign convention, so a positive DQ section and a positive θpx section are picking out the same samples. The published displays call them twin sisters and color them so that the positive side is the bold one, because on a soft-sand play that is where the sands are.

Note that the sign is not measured from the angle. It comes from the slope of the near stack, the same rule module 07 used for the distance and module 05 used for the half isochron. Three attributes, one sign convention, so that any two of them can be crossplotted without one of them being upside down.

3What it separates

And where it stops being trustworthy

An angle is only worth computing if it says something the distance does not. Here is the test: the sand top at a fixed porosity, filled with brine, oil and gas in turn, read both ways.

At 20% porosity under soft shale the angle separates the three fluids cleanly — +6° for brine, +15° for oil, +23° for gas — while the distance moves the wrong way, falling from 0.147 to 0.103 as the sand is charged. A reader with only the distance would call the gas sand the weaker anomaly. Under tight sandstone the position reverses: the angles crowd into a few degrees and say almost nothing, while the distance separates the fluids well.

The failure mode is worth as much as the success. A short vector has a badly determined direction — module 09 made the same point about the Q5 samples nearest the origin — so wherever the distance is small the angle is unstable. Near a class 2 crossover, where the intercept passes through zero, the distance can fall to a few thousandths and the angle swings wildly. Read the two together or not at all.

?Two numbers instead of one

Why the method does not settle for the distance

The AASPI DQ attributes pamphlet calls θpx a crossplot cluster mapping attribute, which names its job precisely. Rock types that sit in different places on the crossplot take different angles, so a section or map colored by the angle shows where each cluster sits in space. That is a different question from how strong the response is, which is what the distance answers.

Module 07 made the point in the abstract: rolling a two-component vector into a length throws away the ratio of its components, and two very different pairs can share a distance. This module is that point made concrete. The distance answers "how big" and the angle answers "which way", and on this model each of them fails somewhere the other works.

Which is the more useful depends on the overburden, which is not something you get to choose. Under a soft shale the fluid effect shows up mostly as a change of direction; under tight sandstone it shows up mostly as a change of length. A workflow that computed only one of them would work in one setting and quietly under-perform in the other, and nothing in the output would say which situation you were in.

Reading a θpx section

The published displays use a two-sided color bar from −90 to +90 in ten degree steps, with the boundaries kept sharp rather than smoothly graded, so that a feature confined to one ten-degree band stays visible. The positive side is colored boldly and the negative side held back, for the same reason as on a DQ section: on a soft-sand play the sands are the positive samples. The two sections are meant to be read as a pair, and the papers describe a flat spot as something that appears on the angle section where the distance section shows nothing in particular.

Module 11 adds the third axis. The signed half isochron of module 05 has been carried along unused since then, and the three together — distance, angle and time thickness — are what the RDQ crossplot actually plots.

Exercises

  1. Watch the fluids separate in angle. On step 3, with soft shale at 20% porosity, read the three angles and the three distances.
    AnswerThe angle gives +6°, +15° and +23° for brine, oil and gas: clean, monotonic, and in the direction you would want. The distance gives 0.147, 0.123 and 0.103 — it falls as the sand is charged, so on distance alone the gas sand looks like the weakest anomaly of the three. That is not an error in the distance; it is what happens when a charged sand's intercept moves toward the origin while its direction swings away from the background. One number cannot carry both facts.
  2. Now switch the overburden to tight sandstone. Read the same six numbers.
    AnswerThe two attributes swap roles. The angles crowd into a few degrees and barely separate the fluids at all, while the distance spreads them cleanly. So neither attribute is the useful one in general — which one works depends on what the reservoir is sitting under, and that is not something an interpreter chooses. This is the concrete version of module 07's argument for computing both.
  3. Find where the angle becomes unreliable. Set soft shale with oil and drag the porosity up to about 28%, watching the distance and the angle together.
    AnswerThe distance collapses to a few thousandths and the angle swings to a large value that means nothing. That is a class 2 crossover: the intercept passes through zero, the vector is almost at the origin, and the direction of a point at the origin is undefined. Module 09 made the same observation about the Q5 samples, which sit nearest the origin and have the least trustworthy angles. The practical rule is the same in both cases: an angle is only as good as the distance it belongs to.
  4. Take the sign off. Untick the box in the panel and look at step 2.
    AnswerEverything becomes positive and the section loses the ability to distinguish a departure on one side of the background from a departure on the other. The magnitude of the fold is a measure of how unusual a sample is; the sign is what tells you in which sense. It is taken from the near stack slope, exactly as the distance and the half isochron take theirs, so all three can be crossplotted against each other without one arriving upside down.
  5. Check the range. Step through several models and watch the folded angle readout on step 1.
    AnswerIt never leaves 0 to 90° before the sign is applied, and never leaves −90 to +90° after. That is guaranteed by the fold rather than observed: each of the four sector rules maps its quarter onto the first one. Bedle et al. (2025b) describe the sector angles as lying between 0 and 90°, which is the unsigned form, and the published color bar runs the full ±90.
  6. Coarsen the sampling. Step the sample rate to 4 ms.
    AnswerFewer samples carry an angle at all, and the ones that remain inherit the coarser quadrant numbering that module 04 measured. Every attribute in this course degrades the same way for the same reason, which is why the papers ask for 1 ms data and sync interpolation up to it before anything else runs.

The main points

  • θpx is the direction of the same vector whose length is the DQ distance, measured after the rotation of module 08 and the split of module 09.
  • The bearing is folded from 0–360° into 0–90° by four sector rules, one per quarter of the crossplot.
  • The sign comes from the DQ trace, not from the angle, giving a range of −90 to +90°.
  • Three attributes share one sign convention — distance, angle and half isochron — so any two can be crossplotted without one being upside down.
  • Zero means the sample lies along the background trend. Ninety means it sits at right angles to it.
  • Under soft shale the angle separates fluids and the distance does not. At 20% porosity: +6°, +15°, +23° for brine, oil and gas, while the distance falls from 0.147 to 0.103.
  • Under tight sandstone the two swap roles. The angles crowd together and the distance separates cleanly.
  • An angle is only as good as the distance it belongs to. Near a class 2 crossover the vector is almost at the origin and the direction means nothing.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Everything upstream is as in modules 03 through 09: the same rocks and picks, the quadrant numbers of module 04, the phase filters of module 06, A and B as the quadrant-enhanced near stack and enhanced far minus near, the baseline rotation of module 08, and the sector split of module 09 applied to the rotated coordinates.

The fold is equation 5 of Bedle et al. (2025b). With θp the polar angle of the split coordinates: for 0° ≤ θp < 90°, θpx is θp; for 90 to 180° it is 180 − θp; for 180 to 270° it is θp − 180; and for 270 to 360° it is 360 − θp. Each result is then multiplied by the sign of the DQ trace. The paper labels the four rules by sector, I to IV.

The comparison on step 3 is made at the sample nearest the top of the sand, and the separation figures are the spread between the brine and gas values as a fraction of the larger of the two. The numbers quoted in the prose are for a 30 ms sand at 40 Hz with 1 ms sampling and no noise; the exercises say which model to set.

One thing this module does not attempt is a field-scale statement about what particular angle values mean. The published Class 3 example quotes a gas response near +25° and a wet response near +60°, which is the opposite ordering to the one this rock model produces, and the difference is almost certainly the model rather than the method — a different overburden, compaction state and baseline would move both numbers. Treat the separation shown here as evidence that the angle carries fluid information, not as a calibration.
