Module 08  ·  The DQ trace

Isochron and half isochron

A quadrant number says where in a loop a sample sits but nothing about how long the loop was. The isochron measures exactly that: the time from one extremum to the next, carrying a sign taken from the slope. Splitting it at the zero crossing gives the half isochron, and the asymmetry between the two halves turns out to say something about how the sand is graded.

Build the rocks

How the sand is graded

A blocky channel sand has both boundaries sharp. A coarsening-upward sand is cleanest at the top and grades into the shale below it; a fining-upward sand is the other way round. The tight streak is a thin cemented bed of the tight sandstone near the base of an otherwise blocky sand.

The wavelet and the data

Work through the three steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references follow.

1The signed isochron

Time between one extremum and the next, with a sign

The isochron measures how long each half loop lasts and which way it runs. It becomes the third axis of the crossplot in module 09, and a way of selecting which part of a waveform to look at.

The isochron is the simplest measurement in the whole method. Take two consecutive extrema on the near stack — a trough and the peak below it, or a peak and the trough below it — and record the time between them. Write that number to every sample in the interval. That is the isochron.

The sign comes from the slope. Going down the trace from a trough to a peak the waveform is rising, and the isochron is positive. From a peak down to a trough it is falling, and the isochron is negative. So a signed isochron of +13 means thirteen milliseconds of rising limb, and −18 means eighteen milliseconds of falling limb. The magnitude is a time thickness; the sign is a direction.

Notice how close some of the isochron values sit to the wavelet's own trough-to-peak time. For a Ricker wavelet that is √1.5 / πf, about 15.6 ms at 25 Hz, and an isolated boundary can return nothing else, because the waveform there is just the wavelet. The isochron only starts saying something about geology where two boundaries are close enough to pull the extrema off those positions, which is the whole subject of step 3.

2Splitting it in half

The zero crossing divides the isochron, and rarely down the middle

Every isochron has a zero crossing inside it, because the waveform has to pass through zero on its way from a trough to a peak. Cutting the isochron there gives two half isochrons, both carrying the same sign as the whole. The four zones that result are trough to crossing, crossing to peak, peak to crossing, and crossing to trough.

The two halves are not equal, and their inequality is the measurement. A symmetric loop splits evenly because the crossing sits midway between the two extrema. Anything that pulls the crossing off center — a stronger reflection at one end of a layer than the other, a thin bed, an interfering event — makes one half longer than the other while their sum stays the same.

The half isochron depends on the position of a zero crossing, and a zero crossing is by definition a low-amplitude sample — the least stable pick on the trace, and the one a strong reflection nearby will move first. Module 02 spent a step on choosing which sample a crossing lands on; this is what that choice was for. Random noise at the signal's own frequency turns out to be a gentle test of that: on this model 40% noise moves the split by about three points. It is interference that moves it, which is what step 3 is about.

The blocky sand splits evenly. The graded sands do not, and the shorter half sits at the cleaner end — at the top for a coarsening-upward sand and at the base for a fining-upward one. A sharper contrast makes a tighter, better-centered extremum, which pulls the crossing toward it and shortens the half on that side. This is the observation Choudhry et al. (2026) build their facies argument on: the sign separates sand from shale, and the asymmetry within the sand separates one kind of sand from another.

The isochron section holds bands rather than wiggles, because every sample between two extrema carries the same number. Where the sand is thinner than the wavelet can resolve, its isochron stops following the bed and settles near the wavelet’s own trough-to-peak time, which is the limit step 1 describes. Gray means no zone was defined there. That happens where the waveform is flat and its crossing falls below the picking floor, so the extremum above it has nothing to pair with; at a few thicknesses that removes the sand’s loop from a whole trace.

?What the isochron is for

An isopach that lives in the seismic cube

Isochron maps are older than any of this. The new part is computing one at every sample of every trace rather than between two picked horizons, so that time thickness becomes a volume you can slice, crossplot and hand to a classifier alongside amplitude. Nothing had to be interpreted first, which is the point.

What the sign adds

In the field examples of Choudhry et al. (2026), sand intervals carry a positive slope and the shales and silts around them carry a negative one. That is not a law of nature — it follows from a soft reservoir under a harder overburden, which is the same convention module 03 enforces with its polarity flip — but where it holds it means a single signed number separates the two lithologies before any amplitude is looked at. Moderate to high amplitude combined with a positive isochron slope is a sand prediction, and machine learning algorithms can use the distinction directly during training.

Uniform where the geology is uniform

A homogeneous layer produces the same isochron reading on every trace through it, because the waveform is the same on every trace. Variability in lithology or thickness produces variable readings, and a real disruption — a fault, a channel margin, an erosional surface — produces erratic and locally large ones. So the texture of an isochron volume is itself the display: flat where the geology is layered, noisy where it is not.

The half isochron adds sensitivity and gives up stability to get it. Peaks and troughs at medium and high amplitude are less affected by thin beds and noise, and they tend to sit at the boundary of a high impedance layer. Zero crossings are low-amplitude samples, and a strong reflection nearby will move them. That is why the half isochron becomes erratic in high-energy depositional settings, and why it is the more informative of the two attributes in quiet, well layered ones.

The isochron is also the third axis of the crossplot in module 09. The DQ distance and Theta PX describe the amplitude behavior at a sample; the signed half isochron describes the geometry of the waveform it sits in. They are close to independent, which is what makes the three of them worth plotting together.

Exercises

  1. Check that the halves add up. On step 2, read the isochron and its two halves for a few models.
    AnswerThe two halves always sum to the isochron exactly, because the crossing is a sample inside the interval and the two pieces are measured to it and from it. Nothing is lost in the split, which means the half isochron carries strictly more information than the isochron and the isochron can always be recovered from it. That is why the papers compute and store both rather than treating one as a summary of the other.
  2. Find out what sets the isochron in a thick layer. On step 1, set the sand thickness to its maximum with a blocky profile, then drag the wavelet frequency.
    AnswerThe isochron zones that belong to isolated boundaries track the wavelet and have almost nothing to do with the sand. For a Ricker wavelet the trough-to-peak time is √1.5 / πf, which is 15.6 ms at 25 Hz and 7.8 ms at 50 Hz, and the measured isochron follows it to within a sample. Note that this is not half a period — half a period at 25 Hz would be 20 ms — because a Ricker's side lobes sit closer in than a sinusoid's would. A layer thick enough that its top and base do not interfere gives you back the wavelet and no more. The isochron becomes a geological measurement only once two boundaries are close enough to interfere, which is the tuning regime module 11 works in.
  3. Split a blocky sand. On step 3, read the blocky panel's two halves.
    AnswerThey are equal. Both boundaries of the sand are equally sharp and equally strong, so the waveform between them is symmetric and the crossing lands midway. That is the baseline everything else is read against — not because blocky sands are common, but because any departure from an even split has to be caused by something, and the something is what you are looking for.
  4. Grade the sand and watch the split move. Compare the coarsening-upward and fining-upward panels on step 3.
    AnswerThe shorter half sits at the cleaner end: at the top for a coarsening-upward sand, at the base for a fining-upward one. The cleaner end has the sharper impedance contrast, which produces a tighter extremum sitting closer to the boundary, and that pulls the crossing toward it. So the asymmetry is a read on which end of the sand has the better rock — the same conclusion Choudhry et al. (2026) draw from their Figure 2, and it comes out of the wavelet rather than out of any assumption about depositional environment.
  5. Try to move the crossing with noise. On step 2, with a blocky 24 ms sand at 25 Hz, walk the noise from zero to 40% and watch the isochron and the split.
    AnswerVery little happens. The isochron drifts from 22 ms to 19 and the split never leaves the high forties. That is worth knowing, because it is the opposite of what the setup invites you to expect, and because it tells you what kind of test this is: band-limited noise at the signal's own frequency perturbs a strong, isolated loop hardly at all. The instability Choudhry et al. (2026) report on field data — half isochron readings becoming varied and sometimes erratic in high-energy settings — comes from strong reflections interfering with each other and dragging the low-amplitude crossing around, not from random noise. Step 3 is where you can see that mechanism working, and this synthetic understates it.
  6. Coarsen the sampling. On step 1, step the sample rate from 1 ms to 4 ms with everything else fixed.
    AnswerThe isochron can only take values that are whole multiples of the sample interval, so at 4 ms it jumps in steps of four milliseconds and the fine distinctions between one zone and the next disappear. The half isochron suffers twice over, because it is a shorter interval measured to a pick that is itself less well located. Every module in this course has ended up at the same place on sampling, from a different direction each time.

The main points

  • The signed isochron is the time between two consecutive extrema, written to every sample between them.
  • The sign is the slope. Trough down to peak is positive, peak down to trough is negative.
  • The half isochron splits the isochron at its zero crossing, giving four zones per cycle: trough to crossing, crossing to peak, peak to crossing, crossing to trough.
  • The two halves sum to the isochron exactly, so nothing is lost by splitting and the half isochron carries strictly more.
  • At an isolated boundary the isochron is just the wavelet's trough-to-peak time, √1.5 / πf for a Ricker. It becomes a geological measurement only where two boundaries are close enough to interfere.
  • A blocky sand splits evenly; a graded sand does not, and the shorter half sits at the cleaner end.
  • The half isochron is the more sensitive of the two, because it depends on the position of a zero crossing, which is always a low-amplitude sample. What moves a crossing is interference from a nearby strong reflection rather than random noise.
  • Where sands are soft and shales are not, the sign alone separates them, which is what makes the attribute useful as a machine learning input.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks and the near stack are built as in modules 01 through 03. The sand is divided into forty sublayers so that a porosity profile can be imposed through it, and a reflection coefficient is computed at every sublayer boundary from the two rocks either side. The blocky profile holds porosity constant; the coarsening-upward and fining-upward profiles hold it constant over the cleaner half of the sand and then ramp it down toward a shale-like value across the other, which is a gradational contact. The tight streak is a tight sandstone bed near the base of an otherwise blocky sand.

The picks are made exactly as in module 02. The isochron is the time between consecutive extrema and the half isochron is that interval cut at the zero-crossing sample between them, both written to every sample in their zone and both signed by the slope, positive from trough down to peak. This reproduces Figure 1 of Choudhry et al. (2026), including its property that the two halves sum to the isochron.

Step 3 compares three profiles rather than four. The tight streak is still a model you can select in the panel, and its effect on the isochron is real, but it is left out of the facies comparison: the direction it moves the split in this model does not match the one shown in Figure 2d of Choudhry et al. (2026), and the likeliest reason is that the published model is built from a real sand log whose contrast, thickness and position this page is only approximating. The published result is taken as the reference, and this model does not yet reproduce it.

The window holds the sand and nothing else. An earlier draft put a bed above and below it so the display would look like a layered earth, and that was a mistake: a neighbor close enough to fill the panel is also close enough to shift the sand's zero crossing, which is the measurement step 3 depends on. With those beds in place a blocky sand stopped splitting evenly, which it should always do. The window is sized to the sand's own response instead.

Noise is band-limited with the same wavelet and scaled as a percentage of the strongest reflection in the model, as in the earlier modules. The isochron is quantized to the sample interval by construction, so a coarse sample rate limits it directly rather than through any modeling choice.

What this module leaves out is the use the signed half isochron is put to as the third axis of the RDQ crossplot, which is module 09, and the relationship between isochron and bed thickness in the tuning regime, which is module 11.
