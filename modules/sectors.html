Module 09  ·  The DQ trace

Sector splitting

One line of arithmetic: after the baseline has been rotated flat, every sample numbered Q3 through Q7 has the sign of its gradient flipped. It is the shortest step in the whole method and the least obvious, so this module does two things — shows exactly what it does to the picture, and shows what depends on it having been done.

What the data does to it

1Where each number sits

The loop, colored by quadrant number

Start from the crossplot of module 07 with the baseline rotation of module 08 already applied, so the background trend lies along the horizontal. Now color every point by its quadrant number rather than by its sign.

The numbers are not scattered. A loop on the trace becomes a loop on the crossplot, and it passes through the numbers in order: the extrema, Q1 and Q9, sit furthest out along the trend, and the zero crossing, Q5, sits nearest the origin with Q3 and Q7 on either side of it. Position in the waveform and position on the crossplot are the same information seen two ways, which is the fact the next step exploits.

Blue counts toward the trough, pink toward the peak, gray is the zero crossing. The dashed line is the rotated background trend, now horizontal.

The samples nearest the origin are the ones nearest a zero crossing, which is what module 03 warned about: a crossing is a low-amplitude sample and the least stable pick on the trace. They are also, on this plot, the ones whose polar angle is least well determined, because a short vector's direction moves easily. Both facts are about the same samples.

2The flip

One line, applied to five of the nine numbers

The rule is short enough to quote in full. Taking the rotated coordinates, if the quadrant number is Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6 or Q7, replace the gradient with its negative and leave the amplitude alone. Q1, Q2, Q8 and Q9 are left as they are. This is equation 4 of Bedle et al. (2025b), in the paper's numbering of Q1 to Q9.

Geometrically it is a reflection in the horizontal axis, applied to the middle of every loop and not to its ends. Because the baseline has already been rotated onto that axis, reflecting in it is reflecting in the background trend.

Notice what does not change: the distance from the origin. Reflecting a point in a line through the origin cannot alter how far from the origin it is, so the DQ distance of module 07 survives the split untouched. What changes is the direction, and direction is the whole subject of module 10.

3What depends on it

The step exists to prepare an angle

Sector splitting is not an attribute. Nothing is displayed from it and nothing is written to a cube. It exists because of what comes next: the RDQ workflow (Bedle et al., 2025b) computes the split coordinates, then takes the polar angle of those coordinates, and only then folds that angle into θpx. Change the split and you change the angle, which changes the attribute.

Below is the polar angle of every sample, with and without the step, plotted against the quadrant number it carries. Five of the nine columns move and four do not, and the ones that move do so by a large amount. Whether that rearrangement is the right one for a given dataset is a question for the papers; what this page can show is that it is a change of direction only, and that the distance of module 07 comes through it untouched.

Each column is one quadrant number. The vertical spread within a column is how much samples with the same number disagree about their angle.

?Four sectors, not two

Where the name comes from

A crossplot has four quadrants, and the DQ literature calls the regions sectors to avoid a collision with the quadrant numbers, which mean something else entirely. The two words are unrelated and the clash is unfortunate: a quadrant number is a position in a waveform, and a sector is a quarter of a crossplot.

The reason for four rather than two is a matter of record. The method's authors used a coarser scheme in the original Phillips work — four quadrant numbers rather than nine, which was enough for 35 Hz data sampled at 2 ms — and it did not include sector splitting. The finer numbering and the splitting arrived together, and the stated reason for the finer numbering was the extra detail it gave the splitting to work with.

Which is a good reason to be careful about the sample rate here as everywhere: at 2 ms there are not enough samples in a quarter cycle for nine numbers to be distinct, and the splitting inherits whatever coarseness the numbering has. Module 04 measured that directly — at 40 Hz and 2 ms, sixteen of eighteen gaps lose numbers entirely.

Exercises

  1. Check that the distance survives. On step 3, compare the two distance readouts, then change the rocks and compare them again.
    AnswerThey are identical every time, and they have to be. The split reflects a point in a line through the origin, and a reflection is a rigid motion about that origin, so it cannot change how far away the point is. That is worth checking rather than assuming, because it tells you the step is doing nothing at all to module 07's attribute and everything to the angle. If you ever see the distance change, the split has been applied before the rotation instead of after it.
  2. Count what moves. On step 2, read how many samples are moved and how many are left alone.
    AnswerRoughly two thirds move. Q3 through Q7 is five of the nine numbers, but they cover more than five ninths of the samples because the interior numbers get repeated in long loops while Q1 and Q9 land on single samples. Module 04 measured that repetition directly: at ordinary seismic frequencies every gap repeats numbers and none omits any.
  3. Check that only the direction moves. On step 3, read how many samples changed angle, by how much, and what happened to the distance.
    AnswerAbout two thirds of the samples change direction, by a large amount, and the mean distance does not move at all. One thing cannot be shown here at all: any statistic computed within a single quadrant number is unchanged by the split, because those samples are all reflected together and a reflection cannot alter the spread of a group about itself. Whatever the step achieves, it achieves it in the relationship between one quadrant number and another, not inside any one.
  4. Look at where Q5 lands. On step 1, read the mean distance for the Q3 to Q7 samples against the whole population.
    AnswerThey sit closer to the origin, because those are the samples nearest a zero crossing and a crossing is a low-amplitude sample. Two consequences follow, and they are the same consequence twice: a short vector has a poorly determined direction, so the angle of a Q5 sample is the least trustworthy on the plot; and a low-amplitude sample is the one most easily moved by noise, which module 03 established when it picked the crossings in the first place.
  5. Turn the step off. Untick the box in the panel and look at steps 2 and 3 together.
    AnswerThe crossplot looks tidier without it — the loop stays a loop — and the angle statistics get worse. The step makes the picture less tidy and the measurement more usable. Anyone meeting sector splitting for the first time and finding it arbitrary is responding to something real, and the answer is that it is not for looking at.
  6. Coarsen the sampling. Step the sample rate to 4 ms and look at step 1 again.
    AnswerWhole quadrant numbers vanish, so the split starts operating on a numbering that no longer distinguishes positions within a loop. The step is only as good as the numbers it is given, and those are only as good as the sample rate. This is the fourth module to arrive at the same place from a different direction, which is roughly how strongly the method depends on 1 ms data.

The main points

  • The rule is one line. After the baseline rotation, samples numbered Q3 to Q7 have the sign of their gradient flipped; Q1, Q2, Q8 and Q9 are untouched.
  • It is a reflection in the rotated background trend, applied to the middle of every loop and not to its ends.
  • The DQ distance is unchanged. A reflection about the origin cannot alter a length, and the module checks it rather than asserting it.
  • The polar angle is what changes, and that is the point: the step exists to prepare the coordinates module 10 measures its angle from.
  • Nothing measured within one quadrant number changes, because those samples are all reflected together. The step acts between numbers, not inside them.
  • Roughly two thirds of samples move, more than five ninths, because interior numbers repeat in long loops.
  • The samples nearest the origin are the Q5 ones, which are the zero crossings, which are the least stable picks and the least well determined angles.
  • Order matters. Rotate first, then split. Splitting before rotating reflects in a line that means nothing.
  • "Sector" and "quadrant" are unrelated words here. A sector is a quarter of the crossplot; a quadrant number is a position in a waveform.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Everything upstream is as in modules 03, 04, 06 and 07: the same rocks, the same picks, the same quadrant numbers, the same phase filters, and A and B taken as the quadrant-enhanced near stack and the enhanced far minus near. The baseline rotation is module 08's, applied as a rotation of the polar angle (Bedle et al., 2025b, eq. 3) using this model's fitted baseline.

The split itself is quoted directly. Bedle et al. (2025b, eq. 4) give it as: if the quadrant number is Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6 or Q7 then the data are replotted using the amplitude unchanged and the gradient multiplied by −1.

The angle spread reported on step 3 is the mean, across the nine quadrant numbers, of the standard deviation of polar angle among the samples carrying that number. Angles are unwrapped about each group's own mean before the standard deviation is taken, so a group straddling 0° is not counted as having enormous spread for that reason alone.

What this module does not do is show the attribute that comes out. Sector splitting produces no output of its own: the split coordinates feed the polar angle, and the angle feeds θpx, which is module 10. Read this one as plumbing.
