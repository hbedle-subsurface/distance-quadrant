What the data does to it

Both pictures come from the same two partial stacks, so neither has more information in it than the other. What differs is how much of that information has been brought to the surface.

On the stack section the sand is a pair of coloured bands with a gap between them, and reading it means deciding which band is the top of the reservoir and which is the base. The bands are the wavelet's response to two boundaries, so their brightness mixes the contrast at each boundary with the thickness between them. That mixing is why amplitude interpretation is careful work.

On the DQ section the same sand reads as one body carrying one value. Every sample between the two boundaries has been given a number, so the interior of the bed is measured rather than skipped.

The sand in this line keeps one thickness and its porosity climbs from left to right, from about 12% at the left edge to about 29% at the right. More porosity means lower density and velocity, so lower impedance, so a bigger contrast against the seal above — and the DQ body brightens along the line to match. Because the number is built from a near stack and a far stack together it also carries offset behaviour: change the fluid in the panel and watch the body change colour while its outline stays where it is.

A second attribute comes with the first and is not drawn here: the angle of the same vector whose length gives the DQ colour. It responds to fluid and ignores bed thickness, which is the pair this course is really about. Module 10 builds it and module 12 measures both against a wedge.
