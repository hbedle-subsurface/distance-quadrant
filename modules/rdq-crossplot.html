1The third axis

What the half isochron is, and what it is not

Module 05 built the signed half isochron and then set it aside. Here is where it is used: the RDQ crossplot carries it as the z axis, alongside the amplitude and gradient that modules 08 and 09 rotated and split.

It is worth being precise about what it measures. The half isochron is the time from a sample to the pick that bounds its half loop, signed positive from trough to peak. That makes it a description of the waveform's shape at that sample — how long the loop is and which way it runs — and not a measurement of how thick a bed is. Once a bed is resolved the loop at its top belongs to the wavelet, so the half isochron settles to a value set by the bandwidth and stops responding to thickness altogether. The panel below shows that directly.

What that is useful for. The AASPI attribute description gives two purposes and both follow from the waveform reading. A section of half isochron shows layers thinning, thickening and pinching out without anyone mapping a horizon, because a loop that shortens laterally is a bed that is thinning. It also separates layers of uniform lithology from layers that are internally variable: a clean, homogeneous bed produces one long regular loop, while a bed with internal contrasts breaks into several short ones, and the half isochron reports that difference sample by sample. And because a shortening loop is what tuning looks like, the same section flags the zones where amplitudes are tuned and therefore not to be read as rock. Merged with a velocity cube it converts to a depth thickness and a gross rock volume.

So this axis is a waveform measure, sensitive to the bandwidth and to interference. It is on the plot because samples from different parts of a loop behave differently, not because it reports the geology directly.

2Three views of one plot

Front, top and side

A 3D scatter is awkward to read on a page, so the DQ papers unfold it into three flat views: the front wall is amplitude against gradient, the plot every earlier module has been drawing; the top view looks down, putting amplitude against half isochron; and the side view looks in from the right, putting half isochron against gradient.

Each view answers a different question about the same points. The front wall says what the AVO behaviour is. The top and side views say where in the waveform those points came from, which is how you tell a cluster made of peak samples from one made of crossing samples.

3Working the plot

Delimiters, polygons and statistics

The crossplot is a tool rather than a picture, and it is worked in three ways. First, every column can act as a delimiter: set a range and only samples inside it are drawn. The half isochron delimiter is in the panel — narrow it and watch the plot thin out.

Second, regions of the plot are enclosed as polygons, defined either by corners in x and y or, unusually, by a pair of polar coordinates — a minimum and maximum angle with a minimum and maximum distance, which is a natural way to draw a region on a plot whose whole geometry is polar. Third, statistics are read back for whatever is enclosed: maximum, median, minimum, the 80th, 50th and 20th percentiles, and a count.

The statistics matter more than they look. They are what turns a cluster on a screen into a number that can be carried forward — into a polygon definition, into a comparison between time windows, or into the look-up catalogues module 11 described. A crossplot you can only look at is a figure; one you can measure is an instrument.

?Why a crossplot at all

And why this one is built differently

A seismic section shows you where things are and a crossplot shows you what they are. Neither replaces the other, and the DQ workflow uses both: the sections of modules 07 and 10 to locate an anomaly, and this plot to characterise it.

What makes this crossplot unusual is that it is built to be worked in polar coordinates as well as Cartesian ones. The baseline rotation of module 08 is a polar operation, the sector split of module 09 is defined by quadrant number, and Theta PX is an angle. So the natural way to enclose a region here is with an angle range and a distance range rather than with a box, and the software has to hold both coordinate systems at once. That is an uncommon requirement, and it is why the workflow needs purpose-built code rather than a standard crossplotting package.

Worth remembering what this module is and is not. It explains the plot and the operations performed on it. It does not demonstrate that any particular cluster separation works on real data, which the papers address and a field dataset would show.

Exercises

  1. Check what the third axis responds to. On step 1, drag the sand thickness across its range and watch the value at the sand top, then do the same with the wavelet frequency.
    HintThickness barely moves it once the bed is resolved; frequency moves it immediately. The half isochron at a sand top is measuring the half loop of the reflection there, and once the top and base reflections separate that loop is a property of the wavelet. Useful to know before reading too much into the axis: it tells you about the shape of the waveform at a sample, not about the thickness of the bed underneath it.
  2. Find the sign change. On step 1, follow the half isochron down through a peak.
    HintIt flips from positive to negative at the peak, because the sign is set by whether the waveform is rising or falling and a peak is where that changes. That is the same convention module 07 gave the distance and module 10 gave the angle, which is what lets all three be plotted against each other without one arriving upside down.
  3. Compare the three views. On step 2, find a cluster on the front wall and then locate the same points in the top and side views.
    HintPoints that sit together on the front wall can be spread across the top view, because two samples with similar AVO behaviour may come from quite different parts of a loop. Without the third axis, samples are grouped by what they look like rather than by where they came from.
  4. Use the delimiter. On step 3, narrow the half isochron band and watch the counts and the statistics.
    HintThe count falls and the median distance shifts, because you are progressively excluding the samples nearest the zero crossings, which module 09 showed sit closest to the origin. Delimiting chooses which part of the waveform to look at rather than filtering out noise, and the statistics readout tells you what that choice cost you in sample count.
  5. Change the reservoir and watch the cluster move. On step 3, switch the fluid from brine to gas.
    HintThe sand samples move as a group while the background stays put. That movement is what the crossplot exists to show, and it is why the workflow crossplots sliding time windows: a cluster that shifts between windows means the AVO class has changed, which is the signal that a new calibration is needed rather than a new interpretation.
  6. Coarsen the sample rate. Take it to 4 ms and look at step 2.
    HintThe plot empties out, because the quadrant numbering that admits a sample to the plot needs samples to number, and module 04 measured how quickly those disappear. The half isochron suffers doubly: it is a count of samples between picks, so at 4 ms it can only take values that are multiples of four milliseconds and the axis becomes a set of stripes rather than a continuum.

The main points

  • The RDQ crossplot has three axes: amplitude, gradient and the signed half isochron, after the rotation of module 08 and the split of module 09.
  • The half isochron describes the waveform, not the bed. It is the length and direction of the half loop a sample sits in, and once a bed is resolved that loop belongs to the wavelet.
  • It is on the plot so that samples can be grouped by where in a loop they came from, which the front wall alone cannot tell you.
  • Three flat views replace the 3D scatter: front, top and side.
  • Every column can act as a delimiter, restricting which samples are drawn.
  • Polygons can be defined in polar coordinates — an angle range and a distance range — which suits a plot whose geometry is polar throughout.
  • Statistics are read back from whatever is enclosed: maximum, median, minimum, P80, P50, P20 and a count.
  • Sliding the analysis window and watching the cluster move is how the workflow detects that the AVO class has changed and a new calibration is needed.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Everything upstream is as in modules 03 through 12. The axes follow the AASPI column specification: for the RDQ crossplot, x is the rotated and split near-quadrant amplitude, y is the rotated and split far-minus-near quadrant gradient, and z is the signed half isochron in milliseconds. The specification gives a starting shale baseline of 330° for the RDQ crossplot and 360° for the plain AB crossplot; this page uses the baseline fitted in module 08.

The delimiter, polygon and statistics behaviour described on step 3 is taken from the same specification, which lists the columns that act as delimiters, the seven statistics to be reported, and the polar polygon definition by two corner clicks. The page implements the half isochron delimiter and the statistics for it; the polygon tool is described rather than built, since it needs an interaction model this format does not have.

The two purposes given on step 1 — mapping thinning and pinch-out without horizon picking, and separating homogeneous from heterogeneous layers — are from the AASPI attribute description, which also notes that the half isochron identifies zones with tuned amplitudes and that merging it with a velocity cube gives a depth thickness and a gross rock volume. This page does not demonstrate those on field data; it explains what the axis measures and how the plot is worked.

What this module deliberately does not claim. It does not show that delimiting on the half isochron improves any particular separation, and it does not reproduce the cluster patterns the papers report from field data. This is a model with one sand in one seal, and the purpose here is to explain what the plot is and how it is worked. Whether a given delimiter helps is a question about a dataset, not about the method.

One measured caution is worth carrying: the half isochron at a sand top does not track bed thickness. Across a wedge from 6 to 54 ms it settles to a constant once the bed is resolved. The axis is a waveform descriptor, and step 1 says so rather than leaving the reader to assume otherwise.
