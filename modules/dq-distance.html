Module 05  ·  The DQ trace

The DQ distance

Two quadrant-enhanced traces go in and one number per sample comes out. The arithmetic is Pythagoras and nothing more, which is worth saying plainly because the name and the build-up both suggest otherwise. What makes the result useful is not the equation but what has been done to its inputs over the previous six modules.

1Pythagoras on two traces

The equation, and the identity hiding in it

Module 04 produced two traces: QN, the quadrant-enhanced near stack, and QF, the far stack read through the same filters at the same samples. Module 01 established that the near stack is the intercept and that the difference between the stacks is the gradient. So write A = QN and B = QF − QN, and the DQ distance is the length of the vector they make:

D = √(A² + B²)

Substituting the two definitions gives the form the papers print, √(2 QN² + QF² − 2 QN QF), which looks like a different and more forbidding quantity and is the same expression rearranged. Worth expanding once by hand: QN² + (QF − QN)² multiplies out to exactly that.

Four traces at the same scale: QN, QF, the gradient QF − QN, and the distance. The distance is never negative, which is why step 2 exists.

Which of the two dominates is not fixed. The difference between the stacks is B multiplied by 0.17 and nothing divides that factor back out, which shrinks the gradient a great deal; but the gradient is often several times the intercept in the rock, and near a class 2 crossover the intercept passes through zero while the gradient does not. Across this model space the ratio at the top of the sand runs from essentially zero to over 3. The distance rolls the two into one number and the ratio is exactly what it discards, which is why module 07 computes an angle as well.

2Putting the sign back

A length has no sign, so one is supplied

A distance is a length and lengths are positive. Left alone, the DQ trace would make a soft sand and a hard streak look alike, since both are large excursions from zero. So a sign is attached afterwards, and it is read from the shape of the near stack rather than from the distance itself.

For AVO classes 4, 3 and 2 the rule is: the peak, the trough, and every sample on a rising limb get a positive value; everything else is negative. The slope is taken from the near stack. Class 1 sands and carbonate plays do not need a second rule, because module 03 already reversed the polarity of both partial stacks before any of this ran.

This is the same slope polarity the signed isochron of module 08 uses, and deliberately so. Two attributes that disagree about which way is up would be no use plotted against each other, and module 12 plots them against each other.

3Blocked, not sinusoidal

Why the output stops looking like seismic

A seismic trace oscillates. The DQ trace does not: it holds a roughly constant magnitude across each loop and steps to a new level at the next one. That change of character is the point of the whole exercise, and it comes from the enhancement rather than from the distance.

Every sample inside a loop was read at the phase that centers it, so every sample reports what its own loop is worth rather than where in the loop it happens to sit. A loop's interior therefore comes out flat. The measurement below is the variation within a loop, as a fraction of that loop's mean: near zero means blocked, and a plain seismic trace scores badly because it swings through zero twice.

The near stack, its Hilbert envelope, and the DQ distance, at the same scale. The envelope is the right comparison, because module 04 showed the enhancement lands close to it.

Be careful about what this does and does not show. The DQ trace is blocked, and being blocked is what makes it readable as layers rather than as wiggles. What the distance has that an envelope has not is the far stack: it is built from two angle ranges, so it carries AVO behavior, and the angle of step 4 carries more of it again. 4The picture it comes from

Distance is a radius, and it has an angle to go with it

Everything so far has treated the distance as an equation applied to two traces. It is better understood as a picture. Plot the quadrant-enhanced near stack on the x axis and the enhanced far minus near on the y axis, and every time sample becomes a point. The DQ distance is that point's distance from the origin, and the equation of step 1 is just Pythagoras saying so.

A point has two polar coordinates, not one. The other is the polar angle θp, measured as a bearing from the positive x axis. The distance says how far the sample is from the origin; the angle says in which direction. Rolling a two-component vector into a single length throws the direction away, which is why the method computes both and why this crossplot — the RDQ crossplot — is what the papers actually put on the page.

Both sets of points are drawn here. The open circles are the conventional pair — the raw near stack against the raw far minus near — which is the crossplot an ordinary AVO analysis produces. The diamonds are the same samples after the quadrant enhancement of module 04. The conventional points sweep in toward the origin as the waveform passes through its zero crossings, because a sample near a crossing has a small amplitude whatever the rock is doing. The enhanced points hold further out, because each has been read at the phase that centers it.

Every numbered sample on the trace, plotted twice. The heavy crimson line is the sample at the top of the sand: its length is the DQ distance and its bearing is the polar angle. The dashed line is the background shale baseline of module 06.

θpx is what module 07 makes of that angle.

A bearing runs 0 to 360°. But the four quarters of the plot carry the same information twice over: a trend and its opposite describe the same rock contrast, once seen at the top of a bed and once at the base. So the angle is folded into a single range of 0 to 90°. Which fold you apply depends on which quarter the point sits in, and the result takes the sign of the DQ trace:

0° ≤ θp < 90° → θpx = θp × sign(DQ)
90° ≤ θp < 180° → θpx = (180 − θp) × sign(DQ)
180° ≤ θp < 270° → θpx = (θp − 180) × sign(DQ)
270° ≤ θp < 360° → θpx = (360 − θp) × sign(DQ)

The readouts below apply that fold to the raw angle, so that you can see what it does. In the real sequence two things happen first: module 06 rotates the shale baseline flat, and module 06 splits the plot into sectors and flips the sign of the gradient for the middle quadrant numbers. θpx is computed from the angle that survives both. Treat the number here as the shape of the idea rather than as the finished attribute.

The conventional points collapse toward the origin between extrema, so most of a trace contributes little to a conventional AVO crossplot no matter how strong the rock contrast is. The enhanced points do not, which is what lets every sample in a bed contribute. Two samples can share a distance and differ completely in angle, and the pair is what carries the AVO behavior. So the method computes an angle as well as a length: the distance answers how big, and the angle answers what kind. 5Against the attributes you know

Intercept, [A+B]/2 and fluid factor, on the same model

Three AVO attributes in common use are computed here from the same near and far stacks, without the quadrant numbering and without the phase rotations. The intercept is the near stack. The gradient is the far minus the near, divided by the separation of the two windows. From those two come [A+B]/2, used as a hydrocarbon indicator by Castagna et al. (1994), and the fluid factor of Smith and Gidlow (1987), ΔF = RP − 1.16 (VS/VP) RS, with RP = A and RS = (A − B)/2.

All four curves share one fixed amplitude scale of ±0.35, so their sizes can be compared directly; the tight bed near 120 ms runs past that scale and is held at the edge. The three conventional attributes are computed from the sinusoidal traces, so they cross zero wherever the traces do, and carry a clean reading only at the peaks and troughs. The DQ distance holds a value through the layer.

The fluid factor also needs a background VS/VP, which is a property of the basin rather than of the trace. The slider below changes it, and the fluid factor curve moves with it while the other three stay where they are.

?Hilbert and DQ, side by side

The same square root over different inputs

The Hilbert envelope is √(A0² + A90²), where A0 is the trace and A90 is the same trace rotated by ninety degrees. The DQ distance is √(A² + B²), where A and B are the intercept and gradient. Identical algebra, and the resemblance is not a coincidence — both are asking how far a two-component vector is from the origin.

The difference is what the two components are. The envelope's components are one trace seen at two phases, so the pair carries information about waveform position and nothing about angle. The DQ pair carries two different angle stacks, so it carries the AVO behavior that the whole exercise is about. The envelope tells you how strong the event is; the distance is meant to tell you how strong it is and how it changes with offset, rolled into one number.

What one number can and cannot carry

Rolling two components into a length throws away their ratio. A large intercept with a small gradient and a moderate intercept with a moderate gradient can give the same distance, and on this evidence they would be indistinguishable. That is not an oversight: it is why the method also computes an angle. Module 07's Theta PX is the direction of the same vector, and the two together carry everything the pair did. Neither alone does.

Because B is B × 0.17 in practice and nothing divides the factor out, the vector is a long way from balanced: the intercept dominates and the gradient tilts it slightly. Module 06's baseline rotation is the first step in doing something about that, and it is the reason the rotation is onto the x axis rather than to minus forty-five degrees.

Exercises

  1. Check the two forms of the equation agree. Read the agreement figure on step 1 for a few models.
    AnswerThey agree to machine precision, because they are the same expression. Expanding QN² + (QF − QN)² gives QN² + QF² − 2 QN QF + QN², which is 2 QN² + QF² − 2 QN QF. The published form is worth recognizing for what it is, because written that way it looks like a quantity with its own meaning rather than a hypotenuse.
  2. Compare the sizes of A and B. Read the ratio at the top of the sand on step 1, then change the rocks and read it again.
    AnswerIt moves a lot. On the default model it is about 1.0, so the intercept and the gradient contribute in similar measure. Under tight sandstone it drops to around 0.2 and the intercept dominates. Take a soft shale over an oil sand at about 28% porosity and it goes past 4, because that is close to a class 2 crossover where the intercept passes through zero and the gradient does not. Across the whole model space the ratio runs from essentially zero to over 3. Two things follow. The 0.17 factor of module 01 shrinks the gradient but does not make it negligible, because the gradient is often several times the intercept in the rock. And the distance discards this ratio entirely: it is a length, and two very different pairs can share one. That is what module 07's angle is for.
  3. Count the signs. On step 2, read how many samples get each sign, then change the sand thickness and read again.
    AnswerThe split stays roughly even — between about 35% and 55% positive across the thickness range — because a waveform spends about as long rising as falling and the sign is read from the slope. That is worth knowing before you look at a DQ histogram: roughly half of any DQ volume is negative by construction, and the negative half is not an anomaly. What matters is the magnitude within each sign.
  4. Turn the sign off. Untick the box in the panel and look at step 2 again.
    AnswerEvery loop becomes a positive hump and the trace stops distinguishing a soft sand from a hard streak. The unsigned distance is a magnitude, and a magnitude cannot tell you which side of the shale trend a sample sits on. The sign is a small piece of machinery carrying a large part of the interpretation, which is why module 08 gives its isochron the same one.
  5. Measure how blocked the trace is. On step 3, compare the three variation figures.
    AnswerThe near stack scores worst by far — 0.63 — because within a loop it runs from an extremum through zero to the next extremum. Before reading a bright DQ body as good rock, note how many things could have produced it: a larger impedance contrast, a change of pore fluid, or simply a bed at tuning thickness. The distance alone cannot separate them, and modules 10 and 12 measure how much each contributes. The envelope scores 0.26 and the distance 0.24, so both are blocked and close to each other. Worth being clear what that does and does not tell you: blockiness is a property of how each sample was read, not of what it was read from, and both were read at the phase that centers them. The difference between the two lives in the far stack and in the angle, not here.
  6. Try a class 1 sand. Set the overburden to soft shale with oil at about 20% porosity and watch the polarity indicator and the signed trace.
    AnswerThe flip is applied upstream, in module 03's terms, so the reservoir top arrives here as a rising limb and takes a positive sign, exactly as a class 3 sand would. Nothing in this module has to know about AVO classes at all. That is the payoff for having done the polarity work at the stacking step rather than here: one sign rule covers every class.

The main points

  • A = QN and B = QF − QN, both quadrant-enhanced, and the distance is √(A² + B²).
  • The published form is the same equation. √(2 QN² + QF² − 2 QN QF) is what you get by substituting and expanding.
  • Neither component reliably dominates. B carries the 0.17 factor of module 01, which shrinks it, but the gradient is often several times the intercept in the rock. At the top of the sand the ratio runs from essentially zero to over 3 across this model space.
  • A length has no sign, so one is attached. Peaks, troughs and rising limbs positive, everything else negative, with the slope read from the near stack.
  • The sign matches the signed isochron of module 08, so the two can be plotted against each other in module 09.
  • One rule covers every AVO class, because module 03 reversed the polarity of class 1 and carbonate cases before any picking happened.
  • The output is blocked rather than oscillating, because every sample was read at the phase that centers it and so reports what its own loop is worth.
  • Blockiness alone does not distinguish it from an envelope. Both score around 0.25 on this model. What the distance has that an envelope has not is the far stack, and the angle that comes with it.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks, the layer stack, the picker, the quadrant numbering and the phase filters are all as in modules 02, 04 and 06. The far stack is formed the same way as the near one, using the mean of sin²θ over 20–30° rather than 0–10°, so that A and B here mean what they meant in module 01. Both stacks are enhanced with the quadrant numbers picked from the near stack, and neither is repicked.

The distance is computed as √(A² + B²) and the published form is evaluated independently alongside it, so the agreement reported on step 1 is a real comparison of two expressions rather than a restatement. The sign is applied afterwards from the sign of the sample-to-sample difference of the near stack, with extrema taken as positive.

The blockiness measure on step 3 is the standard deviation of the magnitude within each extremum-to-extremum interval, divided by the mean of the magnitude over the same interval, then averaged across intervals. It is a coefficient of variation, so it is dimensionless and comparable between traces of different amplitude. Intervals shorter than five samples are skipped.

The filter normalization of module 04's step 4 is applied to the enhanced pair before the distance is formed, as step 6 of the published sequence specifies. Its measured effect on this model is small, for the reasons module 04 sets out, so the blockiness figures on step 3 would be much the same either way.
