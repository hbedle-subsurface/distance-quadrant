Module 06  ·  The DQ trace

Shale baseline rotation

The DQ distance measures how far a sample sits from the origin of AB space. That is only useful if the background rock sits at the origin, and it does not — it sits along a line through it. Rotating that line flat is what converts a distance from the origin into a departure from the background, and the angle you rotate by is not the one the textbooks use.

Work through the three steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references follow.

1Where the background sits

Measured, rather than assumed

Module 01 crossplotted intercept against gradient and drew B = −A on it as a reference line, because that is what AVO crossplotting has used since Castagna and Swan. The background trend is supposed to run along it, and anomalies are supposed to be departures from it.

Here is the same crossplot with the background actually fitted. The brine sand track across the full porosity range is the background: same rock, same fluid, varying only in how much of it there is.

The choice of axes changes the answer. The DQ workflow crossplots the near stack against far minus near, and module 01 established that far minus near is the gradient multiplied by 0.17. That compresses the vertical axis, so the same physical trend reads much flatter than it does in true intercept-gradient space. On the workflow's own axes the background comes out at about −16°, a bearing of 344°. In true A and B it is −58°. Same rocks, same data, two very different numbers — and only one of them is the one to rotate by.

The workflow figure is the one that matters, and 344° is not a number this model invented. The published RDQ crossplots quote field shale baselines at 340°, with rotation examples at 345°, 333° and 330°. Switch the axes control in the panel to see both.

Blue is the brine sand track from 8% to 36% porosity, the solid crimson line is the fit through it, and the dashed gray line is B = −A for comparison. The gap between them is the whole subject of this module.

The number moves very little with the overburden. The two shales give 344° on the workflow axes, within half a degree of each other; the tight sandstone gives 342°, about three degrees steeper, because its own VP/VS is much lower than a shale's and that tilts the gradient. So the direction is mostly set by how the sand's properties change with porosity, with a smaller contribution from the seal. The classical reference line comes from assuming a fixed VP/VS of 2, and real rocks do not oblige.

2Two rotations

Rotating to a convention, or rotating to the data

Rotating a crossplot is just a change of coordinates: the points do not move relative to each other, only the axes do. The question is what you rotate to.

Classical AVO crossplotting rotates so that B = −A lies along the horizontal. If the background really ran at −45°, that would lay it flat. It does not, and on the workflow's own axes it is not even on the same side: the background is at −16°, which is shallower than −45°, so the classical rotation turns it past the horizontal and out the other side. The DQ workflow rotates by the measured angle instead, putting the fitted trend itself on the horizontal.

On the workflow axes the classical rotation makes matters worse: the background spread goes from 0.106 to 0.127, because turning a trend that was only 16° off horizontal by a full 45° leaves it 29° off on the other side. Rotating onto the measured trend takes it to 0.025, a factor of four. Switch the axes to true A and B and the picture flips — there the background is steeper than −45°, so the classical rotation helps by 2.7 times and the measured one by 8. The convention is not simply wrong; it is right about the idea and wrong about the number, and how wrong depends on axes most people never think about. The residue is curvature, which no straight rotation can remove.

3What the rotation does

Departure from the background, not distance from nothing

With the background flat, the vertical coordinate of a point is how far it departs from the background, and the horizontal coordinate is where along the background it sits. Those are two separately meaningful numbers, which is what the unrotated pair was not.

The test is whether a hydrocarbon sand now stands clear of the brine trend. Unrotated it does not: the gas track runs right through the range the brine track already covers, so a large distance from the origin means a porous rock rather than a charged one. After the rotation the brine track collapses onto the horizontal and the gas track lifts off it.

An unrotated distance is measured from the origin, and the origin is not a meaningful place — it is where a rock identical to its overburden would sit, which is nothing in particular. After the rotation the distance from the horizontal is measured from the background, and that is a place with a meaning.

?Why the textbook angle is wrong here

Where minus forty-five came from, and what it assumed

The B = −A line is not arbitrary. It falls out of the two-term Shuey equation when the background rocks have a VP/VS ratio of 2 and the density contrast is small. Under those assumptions the intercept and gradient of a wet rock move together in opposite directions at equal rates, and the background is a line at −45°.

Neither assumption is safe. The VP/VS of a sand varies with porosity — in this model it runs from about 1.75 at low porosity to over 2 at high — and density does contrast. So the real background is steeper, and how much steeper depends on the rocks. The DQ workflow does not try to predict the angle. It measures it and rotates by what it finds.

What this does not fix

  • The background is a curve, not a line. A single rotation lays the average direction flat and leaves the curvature behind. That residue is visible in the numbers on step 2 and is the floor on how well any straight rotation can work.
  • The angle has to be estimated from somewhere. Here it is fitted to a known brine track, which is a luxury. On real data it comes from a wet zone, a well tie, or the densest part of the cloud, and every one of those is an assumption about which samples are background.
  • One angle for a whole volume is optimistic. The background trend changes with depth, with compaction and with lithology. The sector splitting in the second half of this module is partly a response to that.
Worth being clear that this rotation is a change of coordinates and nothing more. It adds no information and removes none. What it does is put the axes somewhere that makes the two coordinates individually interpretable, and that is the whole of its contribution.

Exercises

  1. Measure the background angle under each overburden. On step 1, switch between soft shale, hard shale and tight sandstone.
    AnswerThe two shales land within half a degree of each other — 344° on the workflow axes, −58.4° and −58.8° in true A and B. The tight sandstone gives −61.2°, about three degrees steeper. Most of the direction is set by how the sand's own properties change with porosity, which is identical in all three cases; the residue comes from the seal's own VP/VS, and the tight sandstone's is far lower than a shale's. The practical reading is that one baseline can serve a whole area of similar seals, and that it stops serving when either the reservoir trend or the seal changes character.
  2. Compare the two rotations. On step 2, read the background spread after each.
    AnswerOn the workflow axes: 0.106 unrotated, 0.127 after the classical rotation, 0.025 after rotating onto the fitted trend. The classical rotation makes it worse, because the background was only 16° off horizontal and 45° of rotation carries it 29° past. Now switch the axes to true A and B: 0.62, 0.24, 0.08, and there the classical rotation helps by 2.7 times. The same convention is useful in one plot and harmful in the other, and the DQ workflow uses the one where it is harmful. That is the practical content of "to the x axis, not minus forty-five", and it is a stronger statement than it first looks.
  3. Find out why the spread does not go to zero. Look at the shape of the background track on step 1.
    AnswerIt is curved. The VP/VS trend the sand is built from is a quadratic in porosity, so the track bends, and a rotation is a straight-line operation. Laying the average direction flat leaves the curvature as residue, and that residue is the floor on how well any single rotation can separate background from anomaly. Nothing later in the method removes it either.
  4. Check whether the anomaly actually separates. On step 3, read the two separation figures for a gas sand at 28% porosity.
    AnswerUnrotated, the separation is well below one, meaning the gas sand sits inside the range the brine background already occupies — a large distance from the origin would be telling you about porosity, not about fluid. Rotated, it goes above two. The anomaly was always there; what the rotation does is put it on an axis where it can be seen without the background in the way.
  5. Blur the baseline. Raise the scatter to 30% and read step 2 again.
    AnswerThe fitted angle wanders and the rotated spread grows, because the rotation can only be as good as the estimate of where the background is. This is the limit of the technique on real data, where nobody hands you a clean brine track. It is also the argument for fitting the baseline on as much data as possible and for checking it against a well rather than taking it from the densest part of a cloud.
  6. Try a low-porosity sand. Set the porosity to 12% with gas and look at step 3.
    AnswerThe separation shrinks. A tight gas sand is a small anomaly because there is very little pore space for the gas to be in, so the fluid substitution barely moves the rock. The rotation has not failed; there is less to see. Remember that when a DQ volume looks quiet, because a quiet answer can be a correct one.

The main points

  • The background trend is not at −45°, and where it does sit depends on the axes. On the workflow's near-stack against far-minus-near plot it is at about −16°, a bearing of 344° under either shale and 342° under the tight sandstone; in true intercept and gradient it is −58.5° and −61.2°.
  • 344° matches published field baselines, which the RDQ crossplots quote at 340° with rotation examples from 330° to 345°.
  • The angle is set mostly by the reservoir rock trend. The two shales agree to within half a degree; the tight sandstone is about three degrees steeper, because its own VP/VS is much lower.
  • On the workflow axes the classical rotation makes the spread worse, 0.106 to 0.127, because it turns a shallow trend past the horizontal.
  • Rotating onto the measured trend cuts it by a factor of four, to 0.025, and that is what the DQ workflow does.
  • In true A and B the classical rotation does help, 2.7 times against 8 for the measured one. The convention is right about the idea and wrong about the number.
  • The residue is curvature. The background is a curved track and a rotation is a straight operation, so some spread always survives.
  • After the rotation the two coordinates mean something individually: departure from the background, and position along it.
  • This is what makes a distance interpretable. Measured from the origin it reports porosity; measured from the background it reports departure from the background.
  • The rotation is a change of coordinates. It adds no information and removes none.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks are the same as every module before this: three fixed enclosing lithologies, a brine sand built from porosity through Wyllie's time average and the VP/VS trend of Bedle et al. (2025a), Gassmann substitution for oil and gas, and Shuey's two-term approximation across the interface. This module works entirely in intercept-gradient space and does not build a trace, so no wavelet, sampling or picking is involved.

Two sets of axes. The DQ workflow crossplots the near stack against far minus near, which is what the published RDQ crossplots show. That is not intercept against gradient: far minus near is the gradient multiplied by 0.1701, so the vertical axis is compressed and the background trend reads far flatter. This page computes both, and the panel switches between them. The angles are −15.7° (a bearing of 344°) on the workflow axes and −58.4° in true A and B, for the same rocks. The workflow figure is the one to rotate by, and it agrees with the field baselines the RDQ crossplots quote — 340°, with rotation examples at 345°, 333° and 330°.

The background is the brine sand track. That is a modeling choice and worth being explicit about. A real shale baseline is fitted to a population of wet samples covering a range of lithologies and compaction states; here it is one rock type varying only in porosity, which makes it cleaner and more curved than a real trend would be. The angle it produces, near −58.5° under a shale, should be read as what this particular rock model implies rather than as a number to carry into a field dataset. What survives the modeling choice is the direction of the argument: the background is steeper than −45° and should be measured rather than assumed.

The trend is fitted by ordinary least squares of B on A across the porosity range, and the rotation angle is the arctangent of that slope. Rotating "to −45°" means rotating by whatever brings the B = −A direction onto the horizontal, so that the classical convention is applied properly rather than as a strawman. The background spread reported on steps 2 and 3 is the largest absolute vertical coordinate reached by the brine track after the rotation.

The scatter control adds Gaussian noise to both coordinates of the background points, scaled as a percentage of the track's own extent, and the trend is refitted to the scattered points. It exists to show how the rotation degrades when the baseline is only approximately known; the noise is regenerated only when the model changes, so dragging a slider does not make it dance.

What this module leaves out is the sector splitting of module 06, which is the next thing the workflow does to the rotated crossplot, and the polar angle of module 07, which is measured in the rotated frame and only makes sense once the background is flat.
