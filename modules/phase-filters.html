The phase filters

Every sample now carries a quadrant number. The two traces that come out are the quadrant-enhanced pair everything after this is built on. This is the step that looks like magic and is not.

1Eight copies of one trace

What a phase rotation is, and what it is not

A phase rotation of θ degrees takes a trace and returns x cos θ + H(x) sin θ, where H is the Hilbert transform. For a zero-phase trace that is the same as convolving the reflectivity with a rotated wavelet. The amplitude spectrum is untouched and only the phase spectrum moves.

This is a convolution, not a deconvolution. Nothing is being sharpened, nothing is being estimated, and no information is added or removed. Energy is moved around within each loop: a rotation of −90° turns every zero crossing into an extremum and every extremum into a zero crossing, and the intermediate angles do intermediate amounts of that. Seven filters are needed because Q1 and Q9 both take the unrotated trace.

What this step buys: a version of the trace in which every numbered sample has been shifted so that it sits where an extremum would, which is what lets the two-layer reading of module 01 be applied at samples that were not extrema to begin with.

The addition is worth seeing rather than taking on trust. H(x) is the quadrature trace: the near stack with every frequency component shifted back by a quarter cycle, which turns each peak into a zero crossing and each zero crossing into a peak. A rotation is then nothing more than a weighted sum of the trace and its own quadrature, with the weights cos θ and sin θ. At 0° the quadrature contributes nothing and you get the trace back; at 90° the trace contributes nothing and you get the quadrature; in between you get a blend, and because cos² + sin² = 1 the blend keeps the energy constant.

That difference is not a nuisance to be tidied away — it is the whole subject of step 4, and leaving it uncorrected is what makes the filter bank inconsistent.

2Reading one sample from each

Quadrant enhancement, one integer at a time

Now put the two halves together. Each sample carries a quadrant number from module 04, and each number names a phase. Q1 and Q9 take 0°, Q2 through Q4 take 22.5°, 45° and 67.5°, Q5 takes 90°, and Q6 through Q8 take 67.5°, 45° and 22.5° again on the way back. Read each sample from the filter its own number names, and write the result to a new trace. That trace is QN. Do the same to the far stack, using the numbers picked from the near stack and not repicking, and you get QF.

The interior count restarts after every extremum, so one table serves every half cycle: a limb running from a peak down to a trough takes the same filters in the same order as one running from a trough down to a peak. The angles are applied as published (Bedle et al., 2025a). Step 3 shows what happens to the result if their signs are inverted.

Notice that QN is no longer a seismic trace in the ordinary sense. Neighbouring samples come from different filters, so it is not the convolution of anything with a single wavelet, and its wiggle no longer has a wavelet's shape. It is a trace of amplitudes read at consistent phase positions, which is a different kind of object and is exactly what the DQ distance needs.

3What comes out

Almost the Hilbert envelope, which is the problem

Reading each sample from the filter that centers it is very close to asking, at every sample, how big the waveform would be if that sample were at the middle of an event. That question has a classical answer — the Hilbert envelope — and the enhanced trace lands almost on top of it, within a couple of percent on the default model.

That is not a coincidence. Both are asking the same question, and the enhancement is a coarse, nine-bucket way of answering it. The difference is what happens next: an envelope is a single trace and the enhancement produces a matched pair, near and far, which is what lets module 07 form a vector out of them. Step 4 applies the one scaling the method specifies to that pair.

4Normalizing the bank

The one scaling step the method specifies, and what it is worth

Step 1 showed that a rotation preserves energy and does not preserve peak amplitude. Left alone that would make the filter bank inconsistent: a sample read through the 90° filter would come back systematically smaller than one read through 0°, for no reason to do with the rock. The method corrects it in one line. Each filter is normalized so that the peak amplitude of a three-layer block model is 1.0, and the papers add that power calculations must not be used for the normalization, because what the scheme needs is histograms of peak and trough amplitudes.

The gains are close to one: about 20% at 90°, and under 4% for everything up to 45°. Applied across the trace they change it by roughly 5% of its own amplitude, because the large correction only reaches the zero-crossing samples. That is worth knowing because the step is sometimes described as the thing that stops the DQ trace collapsing toward a Hilbert envelope, and on this model it cannot be: across one loop the envelope varies by a factor of around twenty-five, and a 21% correction on one quadrant does not undo that. Either the description points at something else, or the reference block model is not the untuned one used here. The method tab sets out what is and is not established.

?Why bother rotating anything

The idea underneath the machinery

Conventional AVO reads amplitudes at picked events, which module 01 counted at two samples per waveform cycle. The reason it cannot read the samples in between is not that they are unimportant — it is that their amplitude depends on where in the wavelet they sit as much as on the rock. A sample halfway down a limb is small because it is halfway down a limb, and comparing it with a peak somewhere else is comparing two different questions.

Quadrant enhancement removes that dependence. Rotating the trace so that a given sample sits at the center of an event, and reading it there, gives a number that means the same thing at every sample. Two samples with the same quadrant number have been treated identically, so their values can be compared. That is what turns two interpretable samples per cycle into all of them, and it is the central trick of the method.

Why it is not deconvolution

Deconvolution estimates a wavelet and tries to remove it, which means estimating something that was never recorded and can be wrong. Phase rotation estimates nothing. It is a fixed linear filter with a known, exact response, applied seven times. If the input is relative-amplitude preserved then so is every output, and the whole step is reversible. The cost is that the wavelet is still there afterwards — the method does not sharpen anything — and the benefit is that nothing can be introduced that was not in the data.

The seven filters are applied to N and to F, giving sixteen traces across eight phases, and carrying F − N as well brings the total to twenty-four. That is the working set the rest of the method draws from, and it is worth knowing how ordinary it is: a bandpass-safe filter bank, applied once, with an integer per sample deciding which output to keep.

Exercises

  1. Watch the two weights trade off. On step 1, step the highlighted filter through all eight and read cos θ, sin θ and their squares.
    AnswerAt 0° the weights are 1 and 0, so the output is the trace untouched. At −90° they are 0 and −1, so the output is the quadrature with its sign flipped and every peak has become a zero crossing. In between they blend: at ±45° both weights are 0.707. The squares always sum to exactly 1, which is the whole reason the energy readout does not move — the two components are orthogonal, so Pythagoras applies to them just as it does to the intercept and gradient in module 07. And the sum always matches the rotation computed directly, to machine precision, because they are the same operation written two ways.
  2. Check that a rotation conserves energy. On step 1, step the highlighted filter through all eight and watch the energy readout and the peak amplitude.
    AnswerThe energy does not change at all, because a phase rotation multiplies the spectrum by a unit-magnitude complex number and Parseval's theorem does the rest. The peak amplitude changes a great deal: a 90° rotation of a Ricker peaks at 0.83 of the unrotated one. Energy and peak amplitude are different questions and this step separates them cleanly, which is the whole reason step 4 has something to do.
  3. Find the filter with the smallest peak. Read the relative peak for each of the eight.
    Answer90°, at 0.827 of the unrotated peak. The sequence is 1.000, 0.988, 0.954, 0.899, 0.827 as the rotation goes from 0° to 90° in steps of 22.5°, and back up symmetrically. Those five numbers are the balancing scalars of step 4, upside down: dividing each filter's output by its own peak is what makes the filters comparable. The spread is about 21%, which does not sound like much until you remember it is applied systematically by quadrant number and therefore correlates with waveform position rather than averaging out.
  4. Compare QN with the near stack. On step 2, read the two peak amplitudes.
    AnswerQN's peak is larger — on the default model about 1.12 times the near stack's. Every extremum comes through untouched, because a Q1 or Q9 sample takes the 0° filter, so the enhancement never lowers anything. What it does is lift the interior of every loop toward what that loop's extremum would be, and somewhere on the trace a lifted interior sample ends up above the largest extremum. That is exactly the behavior of an envelope, which sits on or above the trace everywhere and touches it only at the extrema.
  5. Measure how close to an envelope it is. On step 3, read the difference and the correlation.
    AnswerOn the default model, 1.5% of the peak envelope, at a correlation of 0.999. The residual is not error — it is the coarseness of the scheme. Nine numbers across a half cycle means each covers 22.5°, and a sample is assigned one by counting samples rather than by measuring its phase, so it is centered only to within about ten degrees. Switch the overburden to tight sandstone or hard shale and the gap widens to a little over 3%, because the sharper contrast makes shorter loops and the counting is coarser relative to them. Either way the unbalanced enhancement is an envelope with a rounding error on it, and an envelope has thrown away the polarity and the amplitude contrast the whole method exists to measure.
  6. Break it on purpose. Tick the invert box in the panel and read step 3 again.
    AnswerThe difference goes from 1.5% to 21.6% and the correlation from 0.999 to 0.78. Two sign conventions are in use for what "rotate by theta" means; they differ by conjugating the analytic trace. The published angles are written for one of them, and inverting the signs is equivalent to using the other. With the other convention the enhanced trace no longer follows the envelope.
  7. Add noise and see how little the agreement cares. Step the noise from zero to 30% on step 3.
    AnswerThe difference barely moves, from 1.5% to about 2%, and the correlation stays at 0.999. That sounds reassuring and is not. Both curves are computed from the same noisy trace, so a loop invented by noise gets an envelope and a quadrant enhancement that agree with each other perfectly well. The enhancement has no way to tell an invented loop from a real one, because the quadrant numbers it follows were picked from that same trace back in module 03. Agreement with the envelope is a test of whether the filters are being applied correctly, not of whether the answer means anything.

The main points

  • A phase rotation is a convolution with a rotated wavelet. Nothing is estimated, nothing is sharpened, and the amplitude spectrum is untouched.
  • Seven filters cover nine quadrant numbers, because Q1 and Q9 both take the unrotated trace. With N, F and their difference that is twenty-four traces in play.
  • Each sample is read from the filter its own quadrant number names. The result for the near stack is QN, and applying the same numbers to the far stack gives QF.
  • The numbers are picked once, from the near stack, and never repicked. That is what keeps a quadrant number meaning the same thing on both traces.
  • The numbering restarts at every extremum, so one filter table serves every loop and nothing has to be adjusted for whether a limb rises or falls.
  • The published angles are applied as printed. Inverting their signs, which is the same as using the other rotation convention, moves the result from 1.5% off the envelope to 21.6%.
  • A rotation conserves energy but not peak amplitude. A 90° rotation of a Ricker peaks at 0.827 of the unrotated one, and the five distinct values run 1.000, 0.988, 0.954, 0.899, 0.827.
  • The enhanced trace sits within a few percent of the Hilbert envelope — 1.5% on the default model, at a correlation of 0.999 — because both answer the same question about each sample.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks, the layer stack, the near stack, the picker and the quadrant numbering are all identical to modules 03 and 04, so a model built there carries over unchanged.

A phase rotation is applied in the trace domain as x cos θ + H(x) sin θ, with H the Hilbert transform computed through the analytic signal. Doing it on the trace rather than on the wavelet means the same operation applies when noise is present, as it would for recorded data. Quadrant enhancement then reads sample i from the rotated trace named by its quadrant number.

The filter angles. The published table gives one filter per quadrant number: Q1 and Q9 at 0°, Q2 to Q4 at +22.5°, +45° and +67.5°, Q5 at −90°, Q6 to Q8 at −67.5°, −45° and −22.5° (Bedle et al., 2025a). This page applies them as printed, with the rotation defined as x cos θ + H(x) sin θ. Under that definition the enhanced trace follows the Hilbert envelope to 1.5% at a correlation of 0.999. Inverting the signs, which is equivalent to the other rotation convention, gives 21.6% at a correlation of 0.78. The panel offers the inverted version as a switch for comparison.

The Hilbert envelope used for comparison on step 3 is the magnitude of the analytic near stack. The comparison is made only at samples that carry a quadrant number, since the enhancement is undefined outside the first and last pick.

The normalization of step 4 is left out of steps 1 to 3, so that step 3 shows the filter bank before any correction and step 4 shows the correction on its own.
