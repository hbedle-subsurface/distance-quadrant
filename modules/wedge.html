Module 11  ·  The DQ trace

Wedge models and tuning

Module 10 ended by admitting that closely spaced sands contaminate each other. This module takes that seriously. A wedge is the standard way to ask what thickness does to an amplitude, and running one through the DQ workflow turns up a clean division of labour: the distance moves with thickness and the angle does not.

What the data does to it

1The tuning curve

The oldest problem in amplitude interpretation

Thin a bed and its top and base reflections move together until they overlap. Where they overlap constructively the amplitude is larger than either reflection alone; thin further and they begin to cancel. The peak sits near a quarter of the wavelet period, and everything below it is the tuning regime, where amplitude reports thickness as much as it reports rock.

The DQ distance is an amplitude attribute and inherits the problem. Across a wedge from 6 to 55 ms it varies by about a third of its maximum, peaking near 8 ms at 40 Hz and settling to a constant once the bed is thick enough for the two reflections to separate.

2What the angle does

Which is nothing at all

Now put θpx on the same wedge. It is flat. Across the whole thickness range the angle at the sand top does not move — under a tight seal it holds to within a degree, while the distance beside it swings by a third.

The reason is geometric rather than mysterious. Tuning scales a reflection up and down: the vector in the crossplot gets longer or shorter, and both components scale together. A length changes when you scale a vector; a direction does not. So the distance carries the thickness effect and the angle is blind to it.

This is the cleanest division of labour in the method. Two attributes computed from the same pair of numbers, one of which responds to how thick the bed is and one of which does not. Neither was designed for this; it falls out of the fact that a vector has a length and a direction and they answer different questions.

3Two numbers, two questions

And where the division breaks down

Put the two facts together. Module 10 showed the distance tracking porosity. This module shows it tracking thickness as well — and an interpreter looking at a single bright DQ sample cannot tell which of the two caused it. The angle helps, because in module 07 it moved with fluid, and in this wedge it changes little with thickness once the bed is thicker than about a quarter wavelength. Thinner beds shift the angle as well (Bedle et al., 2025b).

So the pair separates two questions that a single amplitude cannot: how much rock is there, which the distance answers but mixes with rock quality, and what kind of rock is it, which the angle answers and which thickness affects much less, except in thin beds.

How much the angle has to say about fluid depends on the seal, and module 07 measured that. Under this tight sandstone the three fluids sit only about three degrees apart — a real separation, and a small one. Under a soft shale they spread much further, but there the reflection is weak and the angle is unstable, which the panel below will show if you switch. The thickness immunity holds generally; how much the angle says about fluid depends on where you are.

The separation of thickness from rock is real, and it holds only where there is a reflection worth measuring.

?Why this is the useful pair

What an interpreter actually has to decide

The question in front of an interpreter looking at a bright amplitude is almost never "how bright is it". It is "is this bright because there is more rock, better rock, or hydrocarbon in the rock". A single amplitude attribute cannot answer that, and no amount of scaling or normalizing makes it able to, because the three effects all move it in the same direction.

The DQ pair does not solve the problem either — nothing computed from two partial stacks can fully separate three unknowns — but it narrows it. Above about a quarter wavelength the angle changes little with thickness, so an angle anomaly more likely reflects the rock or the fluid. The distance carries thickness, so a distance anomaly with no angle anomaly may be geometry. That narrowing is available without a well, and it is weaker in thin beds.

Worth being clear about the limit. The angle separates fluid from thickness; it does not separate fluid from lithology, and module 10 showed it barely separating anything at all under some seals. Read it with the distance, and read both with the module 06 baseline in mind.

Exercises

  1. Find the tuning peak. On step 1 with a tight seal and gas at 24%, read where the curve peaks, then change the wavelet frequency.
    AnswerIt peaks at 8 ms at 40 Hz, and at 14, 11 and 7 ms for 25, 30 and 55 Hz, because tuning is a property of the wavelet rather than of the rock. The classical figure is a quarter of the period, 6.3 ms at 40 Hz; the measured peak sits a little thicker than that because the attribute is read at a picked sample rather than at the true maximum of a continuous curve. What matters is not the exact number but the fact that the peak follows the bandwidth.
  2. Measure how much thickness moves the distance. Read the variation figure on step 1.
    AnswerAbout a third of the maximum across a wedge from 6 to 55 ms. That is the same order as the change module 10 got from varying porosity across its whole range, which is the problem in one sentence: a bright DQ sample could be a thick ordinary sand or a thin excellent one, and the distance alone cannot say. The habit worth forming is to treat a bright sample as having at least three candidate causes — more rock, better rock, or hydrocarbon in the rock — and to ask which attribute could separate them.
  3. Now read the angle. On step 2, compare the two variation figures.
    AnswerThe distance moves by about a third and the angle by about a degree. The angle is not merely less sensitive to thickness, it is essentially blind to it, and for a reason worth understanding: tuning scales the whole vector, and scaling changes a length but not a direction. Any attribute defined as a ratio or an angle of the same pair would behave the same way.
  4. Check that the angle still sees fluid. On step 3, read the fluid separation.
    AnswerAbout three degrees from brine to gas under this seal, against half a degree of movement across the whole wedge. So the fluid signal is six times the thickness signal — but both are small here, because module 10 found this seal to be the one where the angle says least about fluid. The claim worth taking away is the ratio rather than the magnitude: whatever fluid response the angle has in a given setting, thickness will not counterfeit it.
  5. Break it with a soft shale. Switch the overburden on step 3 and look again.
    AnswerThe angle stops being flat and starts swinging, and the distance collapses to a few hundredths. Under a soft shale at this porosity the sand is close to the seal in impedance, so there is barely a reflection, and a short vector has no reliable direction. The separation of thickness from rock is a real property of the pair, but it is only available where there is something to measure. This is the same limit module 07 met at the class 2 crossover.
  6. Thin the bed past the point of no return. Drag the thickness marker down to 6 ms on step 1 and look at the model panel.
    AnswerThe top and base reflections have merged into one event, and module 02 measured what that does to the picks: below about 8 ms the sand interval contains no pick at all. Everything downstream inherits it. The attribute is still defined and still returns a number; what it no longer has is a sample that belongs to the top of the sand rather than to the merged event.

The main points

  • The DQ distance is an amplitude attribute and tunes like one. Across a 6 to 55 ms wedge it varies by about a third of its maximum.
  • The tuning peak follows the wavelet, not the rock, near 10 ms at 40 Hz and moving with frequency.
  • θpx does not respond to thickness at all — about a degree across the same wedge, under a seal that gives a healthy contrast.
  • The reason is geometric. Tuning scales the vector; scaling changes a length and leaves a direction alone.
  • The pair separates two questions: how much rock is there, and what kind of rock is it. How much the angle can say about the second depends on the seal, as module 07 showed.
  • It is a narrowing, not a solution. Two numbers from two partial stacks cannot fully separate thickness, porosity and fluid.
  • The division fails where the reflection is weak. Under a soft shale at moderate porosity the distance collapses and the angle swings, exactly as at module 07's class 2 crossover.
  • Below about 8 ms the sand holds no pick of its own, which module 02 measured, and every attribute downstream inherits that.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Everything upstream is as in modules 02 through 10: the same rocks, picks, quadrant numbers, phase filters, enhancement, distance, baseline rotation, sector split and angle fold. The wedge is modeled by rebuilding the whole workflow at each thickness from 6 to 55 ms in steps of one and reading both attributes at the sample nearest the top of the sand.

This is a two-interface wedge in a fixed seal, not a true wedge section with lateral continuity. The published wedge displays vary thickness across traces and show the section; this page varies it along an axis and plots the attribute, which is the same experiment presented as a curve rather than as a picture. Nothing else in the model changes as the thickness moves.

The tuning peak quoted in the prose is the maximum of the sampled curve, not an analytic quarter period. The two differ by a few milliseconds because the attribute is read at a picked sample and the picking is quantised to the sample rate, so the curve is a stepped approximation to a smooth one. Raising the sample rate control moves the peak around by a millisecond or two for that reason and not because the physics changed.

The claim that the angle is blind to thickness holds where the reflection is strong. It is stated for a tight sandstone seal and shown to fail under a soft shale at moderate porosity, which is the near-crossover case module 07 uses as its example of an unreliable angle. Both statements are measured on this page rather than assumed.
