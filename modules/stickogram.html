Module 03  ·  The DQ trace

The StickOgram

The near stack is now thrown away and replaced by a list of picks: every zero crossing, every peak center and every trough center. Those picks are the acoustic layers, and the quadrant numbers of module 04 get written into the gaps between them. This module builds the picks, works through the rule that decides which sample a zero crossing lands on, and shows what a section made of nothing but picks looks like.

1Three kinds of pick

What survives when the waveform is thrown away

What this step buys: a short list of samples that mark real acoustic boundaries, so that later modules can attach a number to every sample by counting from the nearest boundary rather than by measuring the waveform again.

The StickOgram is also used on its own. Reduced to picks, a seismic volume becomes a set of layer boundaries rather than a set of wiggles, which makes it easier to map horizons, follow faults and outline geo-bodies, and much lighter to feed to a machine learning program. Timeslices through it at ten or twenty millisecond spacing give a first time-structure map before anyone has picked a horizon.

The StickOgram is the center of every peak, the center of every trough, and every zero crossing found on the near stack. Nothing else. The amplitudes between those picks are set aside, the wavelet shape is set aside, and what is left is a skeleton of where the acoustic layers begin and end.

Only the near stack is picked. The far stack is noisier and its extrema sit at slightly different times, and what follows depends on one set of picks being applied to both traces, so that a quadrant number means the same thing on N as it does on F. Picking each trace separately would break that before it started.

Time runs down the page. Red dots are peak centers, blue dots are trough centers, and the open circles are zero crossings. The gray dots are every other sample on the trace, all of which the StickOgram discards.

2Which sample is the crossing?

A rule that only exists because the data is sampled

A zero crossing is a time, and almost never a sample. The trace goes from positive at one sample to negative at the next, and the crossing is somewhere in between. The method needs a sample, because every quadrant number afterwards is written to a sample, so it has to choose one of the two.

The rule is: of the two samples either side of a zero crossing, take the one with the smaller absolute amplitude. That is the sample closer to zero, so it is the closer of the two to the crossing itself. In the quadrant scheme of module 04 this sample is called Q5, and it is the anchor the rest of the numbering counts away from.

One crossing, magnified, with time running down as usual. The two candidates are drawn hollow, the chosen one is filled, and the true crossing is the dashed line. Drag the sample rate in the panel and watch the chosen sample move further from the truth.

The second rule follows from the first. Between each pair of consecutive crossings, take the single largest peak or trough. Usually there is only one and the rule does nothing. When interference puts two extrema inside a single loop — a doublet — only the larger is kept and the smaller is discarded along with the rest of the waveform. A thin bed will do it, and so will a thick sand under a long wavelet, where the top and base loops overlap enough to leave a shoulder. The counter below reports only the extrema discarded between the top and base of the sand, so that it is about the sand rather than about whatever the beds above and below happen to be doing.

This is why the papers specify a 1 ms sample rate and sync interpolation up to it before anything else runs. The rule is the best available choice between two samples, and the way to make it a good choice is to put the samples close together. At 4 ms the chosen crossing can sit two milliseconds from the truth, and every quadrant number counted away from it inherits that error.

?What the picks are for

Two different jobs, one set of picks

The StickOgram has a life of its own as a display, and it is also the scaffolding for everything else in the method. Those are worth keeping separate, because they explain why so much care goes into a rule about which of two samples to take.

As a display in its own right

  • Structure without picking horizons. Every event in the volume is already tracked, in the weak sense that its center is marked on every trace. Time slices through the StickOgram at ten or twenty millisecond spacing give a first time structure map before anyone has picked a horizon.
  • Faults as breaks rather than as dimming. A fault interrupts the sticks. On an amplitude section the same fault is a subtle change in a continuous field, and the eye has to do the work.
  • A much smaller volume to hand a machine. Four numbers a cycle instead of forty. Feeding picks rather than samples to a classifier removes the wavelet from the input, which is otherwise the loudest thing in it.

As the frame for everything after it

Module 04 numbers the samples between the picks from one to nine. Module 05 measures the time between them and calls it the isochron. Module 06 replaces the amplitude at each numbered sample with the output of a phase filter chosen by that number. Every one of those steps counts outward from a crossing or a peak, so a pick in the wrong place does not degrade the answer gracefully. It renumbers everything downstream of it.

The rule is spelled out carefully for that reason, and it is why the papers ask for 1 ms data before any of it runs. The choice between two samples is small. What hangs off it is not.

The picks are made on the near stack and then applied unchanged to the far stack and to the difference between them. Nothing is picked twice. This is what keeps a quadrant number meaning the same thing on all three traces, and it is the reason module 02 spent so long on whether the near stack can be trusted.

Exercises

  1. Count what survives. On step 1, with the default model, compare the number of samples on the trace with the number of picks.
    AnswerBetween 86% and 93% of the trace is discarded, depending on the model. Where there is an event the picks come at four per waveform cycle — a peak, a trough and a crossing on either side — and where there is no event there is nothing to pick, which is most of why the fraction is as high as it is. Module 01 counted two interpretable samples per cycle for conventional AVO, so the StickOgram is not yet an improvement in how much of the trace is usable. It is the same information, reorganized into a frame. The gain comes in module 06, when every sample between the picks is given a value.
  2. Coarsen the sampling and watch the crossings drift. On step 2, step the sample rate from 1 ms to 4 ms, reading the worst crossing error each time.
    AnswerThe error is bounded by half the sample interval, because the rule always takes the nearer of the two samples to zero. At 1 ms that is at most half a millisecond and usually less. At 4 ms it can be two, which at 40 Hz is most of a tenth of a cycle. Everything after this counts samples away from that crossing, so the error does not stay where it started — it shifts the quadrant numbering of the whole loop.
  3. Make a doublet. With the default model — 30 ms of sand at 40 Hz — switch the tight streak on and off on step 2, watching the counter and the dashed circles.
    AnswerZero discarded extrema without the streak, two with it. The thin tight sandstone bed puts a second extremum inside a loop that previously held one, and the rule keeps only the larger. The smaller is a real reflection from a real bed, and the StickOgram does not record it. That is a deliberate trade: the numbering scheme in module 04 needs exactly one extremum between each pair of crossings, and a rule that sometimes returned two would have nothing to number. Now try 45 ms of sand at 30 Hz with the streak switched off, and you will find three discarded extrema with no thin bed anywhere. A long wavelet over a thick sand overlaps the top and base loops enough to leave shoulders on them. Doublets are an interference effect, and a thin bed is only one way to get one.
  4. Thin the sand until it holds no picks at all. On step 1, drag the thickness down from 30 ms, watching the count of peaks and troughs inside the sand.
    AnswerAt 8 ms and below it reaches zero. The top and base have merged into one event, and the center of that event does not sit between the top and the base — it sits above the sand entirely. So the picks record that something is there while saying nothing whatever about its interior, and no attribute computed from them can do better. Module 12 comes back to this: the DQ distance responds to bed thickness where Theta PX does not, and this is where the sensitivity starts.
  5. Add noise and watch picks disappear. On step 1, raise the noise from zero to 40% a few percent at a time, watching the pick count.
    AnswerIt does not climb. With the default model it goes 21, 19, 15, then 13 at 30% noise, before climbing back to 19 at 40%. Noise destroys picks as readily as it invents them: a weak event that was a clean loop stops producing an extremum above the amplitude floor, and a crossing shifts by several samples or merges with its neighbor. Losing a pick is the worse outcome of the two, because a spurious layer can be recognized as noise later while a missing one cannot be recovered at all. This is why module 02 cared so much about the near stack being clean, and why the papers say to bandpass filter for signal to noise rather than for bandwidth.
  6. Find the fault on the wrong display. On step 3, set the noise to 25% and try to locate the fault on the amplitude section, then on the sticks.
    AnswerOn the amplitudes the fault is a subtle lateral shift inside a continuous field, and the noise sits on top of it. On the sticks it is a clean break in a set of lines, because the display has already thrown away everything that was not an event. This is the argument for using the StickOgram as a structural display rather than only as an intermediate product, and it is the same argument for feeding it to a classifier.

The main points

  • The StickOgram is the center of every peak, the center of every trough, and every zero crossing, found on the near stack and on nothing else.
  • Of the two samples either side of a crossing, the method takes the one closer to zero. That sample becomes Q5 in module 04, and the numbering counts away from it.
  • Between each pair of crossings, only the largest peak or trough is kept. A doublet loses its smaller extremum entirely, whether it came from a thin bed or from a thick sand under a long wavelet.
  • The crossing error is bounded by half the sample interval, which is why the papers ask for 1 ms data, sync interpolated if necessary, before any of this runs.
  • The picks are made once and applied to all three traces, near, far and their difference, so that a quadrant number means the same thing on each.
  • As a display it shows structure and faults directly, because a fault breaks the sticks instead of subtly changing a continuous amplitude field.
  • A pick in the wrong place is not a small error. Everything downstream counts samples away from these picks, so a misplaced crossing renumbers a whole loop.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks and the near stack are built exactly as in modules 01 and 02: three fixed enclosing lithologies, a brine sand computed from porosity through Wyllie's time average and the VP/VS trend of Bedle et al. (2025a), Gassmann substitution for oil and gas, Shuey's two-term approximation across each interface, and a near stack formed by averaging 0–10°. The optional tight streak is a four millisecond tight sandstone bed placed just above the base of the sand.

The picking follows the published steps (Bedle et al., 2025a). Zero crossings are located first, as the places where consecutive samples change sign. Of the two samples bracketing each crossing, the one with the smaller absolute amplitude is taken, which is the sample nearer to the true crossing time; that sample is Q5 in the quadrant scheme. Between each consecutive pair of those samples the largest magnitude peak or trough is taken, and where a doublet puts two extrema in one interval only the larger survives. An extremum is taken in the strict sense, as a sample where the sample-to-sample difference changes sign. An amplitude floor of one percent of the largest sample keeps the dead ends of the trace out of the pick list; it plays no part in the method itself and exists here only because a synthetic trace has exactly zero amplitude outside the modeled interval. The count of discarded extrema on step 2 is restricted to the samples between the top and base of the sand, so that it reports on the sand rather than on interference between the beds above and below it.

The crossing error quoted on step 2 is the time between the chosen sample and the true crossing, found by linear interpolation between the two bracketing samples. It is bounded above by half the sample interval, which is a property of the rule rather than of the data.

The layer stack is a contrasting bed from 14 to 26 ms, the sand from 50 ms down, an optional four millisecond tight sandstone streak three quarters of the way through it, and a tight bed from 118 to 130 ms. Structure moves the whole stack together. The extra beds are there so that the trace looks like something worth picking rather than like a lone reflection in an empty window.

The section on step 3 is seventy traces. The top of the sand follows a gentle anticline, the sand thins linearly to the right, and a normal fault at the fortieth trace displaces everything below it by the throw set in the panel. Each trace is modeled and picked independently; nothing is smoothed or tracked laterally, so the continuity in the display is the continuity in the model rather than something the picker imposed.

Noise is band-limited with the same wavelet, drawn independently for each trace, and scaled as a percentage of the strongest reflection in the model. Where the sample rate is coarser than 1 ms the trace is modeled directly at that rate rather than decimated, which flatters the coarse cases slightly, since real decimation would alias as well.

What this module leaves out is the polarity convention. For AVO classes 4, 3 and 2 the trough is Q1 and the peak is Q9. For class 1 sands and carbonate plays the polarity of both partial stacks is reversed at the stacking step instead, so that the numbering scheme never has to change. Module 04 takes that up.
