Module 11  ·  The DQ trace

DQ and porosity

Ten modules of machinery have produced a number. This is where it earns its keep: the DQ distance tracks the porosity of a sand about as tightly as acoustic impedance does, without a well tie, a wavelet estimate or an inversion. It also does so with a sign that belongs to the overburden and not to the method, and that second fact is as important as the first.

1What porosity does to the rock

Before any seismic is involved

This is where the DQ trace earns the description the AASPI DQ attributes pamphlet gives it: before any inversion, it is a relative porosity curve. Relative because the vertical axis is in amplitude units and nothing here converts them to porosity units; a curve, because it varies down the trace with the rock rather than reporting a single number per layer. After an inversion tied to wells it becomes a quantitative prediction of porosity or hydrocarbon pore volume, which is the third paper.

Start with no seismic at all. Porosity is the fraction of a rock's volume that is pore space rather than mineral grains. Raising it replaces quartz, which is dense and fast, with brine or gas, which are lighter and slower. So the bulk density falls, and the P-wave velocity falls with it because sound travels more slowly through the fluid than through the frame. Acoustic impedance is velocity multiplied by density, so both terms push the same way and the impedance falls steadily.

The VP/VS ratio moves the other way. Pore fluid resists compression but not shear, so adding porosity weakens the rock against a P-wave more than against an S-wave, and the ratio of the two velocities rises. Both relationships are close to monotonic here, and both belong to the sand alone — nothing above it enters into either.

Neither of these is measurable from seismic without work. Acoustic impedance needs an inversion, which needs a wavelet and usually a well. VP/VS needs shear information, which needs either converted waves or a simultaneous inversion. The question the next step asks is how close the DQ distance gets to the same information with neither.

2What it does to the DQ trace

The same curve, from seismic amplitudes alone

Now run the whole workflow. For each porosity in turn, build the sand, model the near and far stacks, pick, number, filter, enhance, and read the DQ distance at the top of the sand. No wavelet has been estimated, no well has been used and nothing has been inverted.

Under a tight sandstone seal the result is a near-straight line. The distance rises with porosity at a correlation of about 0.99, against 0.99 for acoustic impedance and 1.00 for VP/VS — the same information, from amplitudes.

The sequence of causes is worth following once, because the rest of the module depends on it. Porosity raises pore volume, which lowers density and velocity, which lowers the sand's impedance. The seal's impedance does not change, so the contrast across the top of the sand grows. A larger contrast gives a larger reflection coefficient at both the near and the far angle, which gives a longer vector on the crossplot, which gives a larger DQ distance. Each link is ordinary rock physics or ordinary reflection theory; the attribute carries the result through to a number you can map.

Read the correlation for what it is. It is measured on one modeled sand at a time under a fixed seal, with no shale content and no lithological variation, so it is an upper bound rather than an estimate of field performance. What it establishes is that the information is present in the attribute.

It does not yet give a porosity value: the vertical axis is in amplitude units and the conversion from one to the other is a slope and an intercept that nothing here supplies. A well tie supplies them, and that is the subject of the third of the DQ papers rather than of this course.

3The sign belongs to the seal

The result that decides how the method is used

Change the rock above the sand and the relationship changes character. Under tight sandstone the distance rises with porosity. Under a soft shale it falls, just as cleanly, because a porous sand approaches the shale's own impedance and the contrast between them dies away entirely in the mid thirties. Under a hard shale the contrast passes through zero in the middle of the range and the relationship is a V, which no single correlation describes at all.

The sand is identical in all three. Nothing about the reservoir has changed, and the attribute has reversed its sign. Whatever relationship holds between the DQ distance and porosity, it belongs to the interval and not to the method.

4Recovering a porosity log

One synthetic trace, five sands, a known answer

The previous steps compared one model against another. This one does the test properly: a single synthetic, a porosity log we chose, and a DQ trace computed from the seismic with no knowledge of it. Five sand bodies of different porosity, each encased in the same seal, with shale between them so that every sand's top is a contrast against the same rock.

Read the DQ trace at each sand top and compare it with the porosity that built that sand. Under a tight sandstone seal the two track each other at a correlation of 1.00, and the five readings rank the five sands in the right order; under a soft shale the correlation is −1.00, the same ranking reversed. In this noise-free, single-mineral model the log is recovered up to a sign and a scale that step 3 explained and that only a well can supply. With real data, noise, mixed lithology, fluid changes and bed thickness all move the DQ value as well, so the match is weaker and a bright DQ value has more than one possible cause.

The spacing matters more than it looks. These sands sit 62 ms apart, so the base of one is more than a wavelength from the top of the next and each reflection stands on its own. Pack them to 24 ms and the correlation falls to about 0.8, because the value read at a sand top is then contaminated by its neighbors rather than describing its own rock. That is a limit on the seismic, not on the attribute, and module 12 takes it up in earnest.

One thing this does not show, and it matters. The sands here are separated by shale, so each top is a contrast against a fixed rock. Stack sands directly on one another and the DQ trace responds to the porosity step between them rather than to porosity itself, because a reflection is a contrast and never an absolute. That is a real limit on what any amplitude attribute can do, and it is why a DQ section reads a layered sequence well and a gradational one badly.

?What this is and is not

Detection, calibration, and the line between them

The DQ papers separate two things. Detection asks where something interesting is, and does not need a well: the earlier modules are all detection, and so is the correlation on step 2. Quantitative prediction asks how much porosity is there, in porosity units, and does need a well, because the conversion from an amplitude to a rock property has a slope and an intercept that only a calibration can supply.

The method's authors are explicit that this ordering was historical as well as logical. The original work was built for quantitative inversion and required forward modeling from full well log suites; the detection attributes were a by-product that turned out not to need any of it. Bedle et al. (2025a, 2025b) describe the detection attributes, and Bedle et al. (2025c) returns to quantitative inversion.

Why one catalog is never enough

Step 3 shows the sign flipping between seals. In a real basin the same thing happens with depth: compaction moves both the sand trend and the shale trend, and where they cross, the relationship inverts. The reported field practice matches — two catalogs over a two-second window in one offshore dataset, a forced catalog switch at an unconformity in another where the petrophysics changed abruptly, and a single catalog sufficing in a third field where it did not. None of that is a weakness of the attribute. It is what any amplitude-based rock property estimate has to live with, and the DQ workflow at least makes the boundary visible by letting you crossplot a time window and watch the cluster move.

The honest summary of this module: the DQ distance sees the rock. It sees it through the contrast with whatever is above it, which is true of every reflection attribute ever devised, and the practical consequence is that you calibrate per interval rather than once.

Exercises

  1. Read the correlation under tight sandstone. On step 2, with a tight sandstone seal and brine fill, read the two correlation figures.
    AnswerAbout 0.99 against porosity and about 1.00 against acoustic impedance. In this model the attribute, computed from partial-stack amplitudes alone with no wavelet estimate and no well, follows porosity about as closely as impedance does. The model has one mineral, one seal and no noise, so the figure is an upper limit. The attribute carries porosity information; it does not deliver a porosity number, and step 2's legend explains why.
  2. Now switch to a soft shale seal. Read the same figure.
    AnswerAbout −0.97. The correlation is nearly as strong and points the other way. A porous sand under a soft shale is acoustically similar to the shale, so the reflection weakens as porosity rises, and the distance falls. The rock has not changed; the seal has. Anyone who calibrated a DQ-to-porosity relationship under tight sandstone and applied it under shale would have the sign backwards.
  3. Find where the contrast dies. Still on soft shale, look at where the curve bottoms out on step 3.
    AnswerIn the mid thirties, where the distance falls to under a hundredth — on the 2% sampling this page uses, the minimum lands at 34%. That is the class 2 crossover: the sand and the shale have matched impedances, there is almost no reflection, and the attribute has nothing to measure. Module 10 used the same region as its example of an unreliable angle, for the same underlying reason. Near a crossover, every attribute in this course is weak at once.
  4. Try the hard shale. Read its correlation on step 3.
    AnswerAbout 0.43, which is not a useful number and is not meant to be. The crossover sits in the middle of the porosity range rather than at one end, so the curve is a V and a straight-line correlation averages the two halves into nonsense. This is a good reason to look at the shape before trusting a coefficient — the correlation is low here not because the physics is noisy but because it is two-sided.
  5. Change the fluid. Switch between brine, oil and gas under tight sandstone.
    AnswerThe curve shifts but keeps its shape and its sign. Which means a single DQ value is ambiguous: a brine sand at high porosity and a gas sand at lower porosity can give the same number. Porosity and fluid both move the distance the same way here, and only the angle of module 10 tells them apart. Hydrocarbon lowers the sand's impedance, which under a tight sandstone seal increases the contrast and lifts the whole curve. So fluid and porosity both move the distance in the same direction here, and the distance alone cannot separate them. That is exactly what module 10's angle is for, and it is the clearest argument on the site for computing both attributes rather than one.
  6. Add noise. Take the noise to 30% under tight sandstone and watch the correlation.
    AnswerIt barely moves, and then it gives way. The correlation stays around 0.98 or better all the way to about 35% noise, and then drops sharply — to a little over 0.5 by 40% on the brine case, and further under other fluids. That is a cliff rather than a slope, and the reason is the picking rather than the amplitudes: while the picks stay in the right places the trend survives almost intact, and once noise starts inventing and destroying picks — which module 03 measured directly — the workflow downstream has nothing sound to work with. The practical reading is that this holds up in ordinary data and is worth no trust at all in bad data, with less warning in between than you would like. Treat the figure as an upper bound in any case: it is computed on one modeled sand at a time, not on a population of real samples.

The main points

  • In this model the DQ distance tracks porosity about as closely as acoustic impedance does, from partial-stack amplitudes with no wavelet estimate, no inversion and no well. Real data, with noise, mixed lithology and varying thickness, give a weaker relationship.
  • Under a tight sandstone seal the correlation is about +0.99 against porosity, and about 1.00 against impedance.
  • Under a soft shale it is about −0.97. The relationship is just as strong and points the other way.
  • Under a hard shale it is about 0.43, because the crossover falls mid-range and the curve is a V that no single correlation describes.
  • The sand is identical in all three. The attribute reads the contrast, and the contrast belongs to the pair.
  • Near a crossover every attribute is weak at once — the distance collapses and the angle of module 10 becomes meaningless together.
  • Fluid and porosity move the distance the same way, so the distance alone cannot separate them. The angle can.
  • This is detection, not prediction. Turning a distance into a porosity value needs a slope and an intercept, and those come from a well.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Everything upstream is as in modules 03 through 10, unchanged: the same rocks, picks, quadrant numbers, phase filters, enhancement, distance, baseline rotation and sector split. The distance is read at the sample nearest the top of the sand, and the curve is built by rebuilding the whole workflow at each porosity from 10% to 36% in steps of two.

Acoustic impedance and VP/VS on step 1 are properties of the modeled sand and involve no seismic: impedance is velocity times density, and both come from Wyllie's time average and the volumetric density mix with the VP/VS trend of Bedle et al. (2025). Correlations are ordinary Pearson coefficients across the porosity samples.

What the correlation figure does and does not represent. It is computed on one modeled sand at a time under a fixed seal, with no lithological variation, no shale content and no noise unless you add it. A real correlation on a real basin would be lower, and the number here should be read as a demonstration that the information is present in the attribute rather than as an estimate of how well it would perform. The step 3 result — that the sign depends on the seal — is the finding that holds generally, because it follows from the sign of an impedance contrast rather than from any detail of the rock model.

The module stops short of predicting a porosity value, deliberately. The vertical axis is in amplitude units, and the conversion is a scaling that has to be calibrated. The method's authors treat that as a separate paper requiring forward modeling from well logs, and this course covers the detection side only.
