Module 14  ·  The DQ trace

The attributes on a line

Every module so far has worked on one trace or one model at a time. This one puts a geological structure under the whole workflow: a dipping section cut by a normal fault, a sand that thickens and thins with its porosity rising alongside, and a fluid contact you can switch on. Each attribute the course has built is then shown on it, including the two layer attributes that have not appeared before.

1Structure on both displays

The same geology, imaged two ways

The model is a dipping section cut by a normal fault about halfway along, with a sand body that is thin at both ends and thick in the middle. Its porosity rises and falls with its thickness, which is a common enough pairing: the middle of a channel or a bar is usually both thicker and cleaner than its edges.

Both displays show the same rocks. On the stack section the sand is a pair of bands and its extent has to be inferred from where those bands brighten. On the DQ section it is a single body whose color follows the rock. The fault and the dip are equally clear on both, because both are built from the same reflections.

The dashed outline is the modeled sand, so each display can be checked against the geology that produced it.

2Angle and time thickness

Two attributes that answer different questions

θpx maps where each cluster of rock sits on the crossplot, so on a section it separates the sand from the background by kind rather than by strength. The half isochron measures how long each half loop lasts, so it responds to bed thickness and to whether a layer is internally uniform.

Switch the fluid contact on in the panel. It is placed at a fixed time, so it cuts flat across the dipping, faulted sand — the geometry of a real hydrocarbon contact. Watch where it appears and where it does not.

A boundary that stays flat while the beds around it dip is the classic argument for a fluid contact rather than a lithology change, because rock boundaries follow structure and fluid boundaries follow gravity. That reasoning is independent of which attribute you are looking at; the attribute only decides how visible it is.

3Two layer attributes

DQ Average and DQ Sum

Everything so far has been a value per sample. These two are values per layer. Take all the DQ samples between one extremum and the next — that is, everything inside a single trough-to-peak or peak-to-trough loop — and either average them or add them up. Both numbers are then written to every sample in that layer, so the result still displays as a section.

They answer different questions. DQ Average describes the quality of the rock in the layer, because averaging removes how many samples there happen to be. DQ Sum keeps that count, so it grows with both quality and thickness. Neither escapes tuning: both are built from DQ values, and where a bed is thin enough to tune, those values are amplified before either is computed. In the AASPI DQ attributes pamphlet these become average and total relative porosity for a layer, and after a well tie, average and total hydrocarbon pore volume. The pamphlet suggests targeting horizontal wells at zones where both the average and the sum are high.

Both curves peak toward the right-hand end, where the sand is thinnest and its porosity is lowest. That is not a fault in the attributes and it is worth understanding: at 10 ms those beds sit at the tuning maximum module 11 measured, so their reflections are amplified by interference. An interpreter reading either section for rock quality would pick the worst part of the reservoir. The defence is the half isochron on step 2, which shows the loop shortening there and marks the zone as tuned.

?The attributes, and where they came from

A short catalog

This is the point of the course, so it is worth setting the attributes out together with what each measures and where in the workflow it appears.

StickOgram (module 02) — the centers of peaks and troughs and the zero crossings of the near stack. A volume reduced to layer boundaries, used for mapping horizons and faults and as light input to machine learning.
Quadrant number (module 03) — where a sample sits within its loop, one of nine. Not usually displayed; it is what tells the enhancement which filter to read each sample from.
Isochron and half isochron (module 08) — the time thickness of a loop and of a half loop, signed by slope. Maps thinning and pinch-out without horizon picking, separates uniform from internally variable layers, and flags tuned zones.
Near Optimized and Far-Near Optimized (module 04) — the quadrant-enhanced near stack and gradient. These are the two traces everything downstream is built from, and they are displayed in their own right when clusters are being defined.
DQ distance (module 05) — the distance from the crossplot origin to the sample. Before an inversion it is a relative porosity curve; after one, a quantitative prediction of porosity or hydrocarbon pore volume.
θpx (module 07) — the polar angle of the same point, folded and signed. A crossplot cluster mapping attribute: it says which kind of rock, where the distance says how much response.
DQ Average and DQ Sum (this module) — the mean and total of DQ within one layer. Average and total relative porosity, and after a well tie, average and total hydrocarbon pore volume.

Exercises

  1. Find the fault on each display. Step through the six displays in the panel and look for the offset.
    AnswerIt is visible on all of them, because a fault offsets the reflections themselves and every attribute here is computed from those reflections. What differs is how sharply it reads: the half isochron often shows it most clearly, because the loop structure changes abruptly across the offset. No attribute here adds structural information that was not already in the stack.
  2. Compare the thin ends with the middle. On step 1, look at where the sand is thinnest.
    AnswerThe outline no longer matches a distinct body, and the DQ brightness there is tuning rather than rock quality. This is module 11's finding shown as a picture. A student who reads the thin end as poor rock and the middle as good rock happens to be right on this model, because porosity was built to rise with thickness — but they would have been right for the wrong reason, and on a model where the two were opposed they would have been wrong.
  3. Turn on the fluid contact. Then look at step 2.
    AnswerA boundary appears that stays at one time while the beds around it dip and step across the fault. That flatness is the argument for a fluid contact rather than a lithology change: rock boundaries follow structure, fluid boundaries follow gravity. Note also where it stops — it can only show where the sand is present and thick enough to contain it.
  4. Compare DQ average with DQ sum. On step 3, read where each peaks.
    AnswerBoth peak near the thin right-hand end rather than in the thick, porous middle, because a 10 ms bed at 40 Hz is close to the tuning maximum and its reflections are amplified. The lesson is the one module 11 makes numerically, seen here as a picture: a layer attribute inherits every problem the samples it is built from already had. Raise the wavelet frequency to 70 Hz, so the thin end is resolved, and the sum moves to trace 14 — next to the thickest part of the sand, which is where the volume is. The average moves less, because at the thin end the top and base reflections of a short loop still reinforce each other even when the bed is nominally resolved. The average is about rock quality and the sum multiplies that by how much rock there is — but only once you are out of the tuning regime.
  5. Change the fluid. Switch between brine, oil and gas and watch the DQ and angle sections.
    AnswerThe body changes value while its outline stays put, because the geometry has not changed. How much it changes depends on the seal, which module 10 measured: under some seals the fluid effect is mostly a change of distance, under others mostly a change of angle. Try all three overburdens and watch which of the two displays does the work.
  6. Add noise. Take it to 30% and look at each display in turn.
    AnswerThe angle and half isochron sections degrade first, because both depend on the picks being in the right places and noise moves picks. The DQ section holds up longer because it is an amplitude. When displays that should agree start disagreeing, suspect the picking rather than the rock.

The main points

  • Every attribute here is computed from the same two partial stacks, so none of them adds structural information the stack section did not have.
  • The DQ section shows a bed as one body where the stack section shows two bands.
  • θpx maps clusters, so it separates rock types; the distance measures response strength.
  • The half isochron responds to loop length, which makes it good at thinning, pinch-out and tuned zones.
  • DQ Average is a value per layer describing rock quality, and inherits any tuning in the samples it averages.
  • DQ Sum keeps the sample count, so it grows with quality and thickness together, and it is the one that matters for volume.
  • A flat boundary cutting across dip suggests a fluid contact, because rock boundaries follow structure and fluid boundaries follow gravity.
  • Where a bed is thin, brightness is tuning on every one of these displays.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The line is thirty-one traces. Each carries a regional dip of 26 ms across the line and a normal fault at trace 18 with a throw of 14 ms. The sand runs from 10 ms thick at both ends to 36 ms in the middle, and its porosity follows the same shape from 14% to 32%. Two thin background beds above and below give the section something other than the reservoir to look at. Everything upstream is as in modules 02 through 09, applied independently to each trace.

The fluid contact, when switched on, is placed at a fixed 98 ms so that it cuts flat across the dip and the fault. The chosen fluid fills the sand above it and brine below. It is drawn only where it falls inside the sand, which is why it stops before the thin ends.

DQ Average and DQ Sum follow the AASPI definition: the arithmetic mean and the arithmetic sum of the DQ values between one extremum and the next, so a single trough-to-peak or peak-to-trough layer. Both are written back to every sample in that layer so they can be displayed as sections. The curves below the sections read the layer that contains the middle of the modeled sand.

What this module does not do. It does not ray trace, and each trace is modeled independently, so there is no lateral wave propagation, no diffraction from the fault tip and no migration artefact. A real fault on real data produces all three. The structure here is a device for showing how the attributes behave when geology varies along a line, not a simulation of a seismic experiment.
