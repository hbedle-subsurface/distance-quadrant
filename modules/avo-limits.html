Module 01  ·  The DQ trace

Where AVO runs out

AVO theory describes a single boundary between two thick layers. Seismic data is a stack of many thin layers convolved with a wavelet. The gap between those two statements is small enough to ignore at a bright top-of-sand pick and large enough to invalidate everything else on the trace. This module makes the gap visible, because the whole DQ method exists to close it.

1One boundary, no wavelet

The picture AVO theory actually describes

Start with the simplest possible case: an infinitely thick overburden resting on an infinitely thick sand, and a single boundary between them. Send a wave down at some angle and part of it comes back. How much comes back depends on the angle, and that dependence is the entire content of AVO.

Two terms first. Acoustic impedance is a rock's P-wave velocity multiplied by its density; a reflection happens where impedance changes across a boundary, and the bigger the change, the stronger the reflection. The reflection coefficient is the fraction of the incoming amplitude that comes back, so it is a number between −1 and +1 whose sign says whether the rock below is faster and denser than the rock above, or slower and lighter.

Shuey's approximation writes the angle dependence as a straight line in sin²θ: R(θ) = A + B sin²θ. A is the intercept, the reflection coefficient the boundary would give at zero angle, controlled mostly by the impedance contrast. B is the gradient, how fast the reflection changes as the angle opens up. The gradient depends on how VP/VS changes across the boundary, and because gas lowers a sand's VP/VS much more than it lowers its density, the gradient is where a fluid change usually shows up.

There is no button for AVO class in the panel above. You set three things — what the overburden is, how porous the sand is, and what fluid fills its pores — and the class is whatever those three choices produce. Drag the porosity slider and watch the class label change without you having asked for it.

That ordering is the point. The class comes out of the contrast between the sand and the rock above it, so it belongs to the pair rather than to the reservoir. Everything you are setting by hand here — the shale properties, the porosity, the fluid — is something nobody knows at the start of a project, which is exactly where the DQ workflow is meant to be used.

The shaded bands are the two angle ranges the DQ workflow uses: 0–10° for the near stack and 20–30° for the far stack. Averaging the curve inside each band gives one number per band, marked on the curve.

2What the wavelet does to it

Same sand, now with a base and a wavelet

Give the sand a base as well as a top and you have two reflection coefficients instead of one, equal and opposite. Convolve them with a wavelet and you no longer have two numbers on the trace — you have a smooth waveform spread over tens of milliseconds, and every sample in it is a mixture of both boundaries. That is the trace shown above.

Now crossplot it. Take the near-stack amplitude at every sample as the x value and the far-minus-near difference at every sample as the y value, exactly as an AB crossplot would. The single point from step 1 spreads out into a long streak.

The y axis here is the raw difference between the two stacks, which is what the DQ workflow uses as its gradient. It is not the gradient of step 1 to scale. Subtracting the near stack from the far stack leaves B multiplied by the difference in mean sin²θ between the two angle bands, and that factor is only 0.17. The streak is wide and flat for that reason and not because the sand is unusual: on square axes, a gradient always draws seventeen times smaller than an intercept of the same size. Everything from module 06 onward has to cope with that, and the RDQ crossplot's rotation is one of the ways it does.

Every sample in the window is plotted. The crimson cross marks the two-layer answer from step 1 — where all of this data ought to be if the theory described the data. The axes are the same ones step 1 used and do not rescale, so the streak really does shrink and grow as you change the model. Drag the thickness slider and watch it breathe.

Polar angle below means the direction of a point from the origin of the crossplot, measured as a bearing. The spread is how much that direction varies across the samples: zero means every sample lies along one line through the origin. It becomes an attribute in its own right in module 05.

With the underburden following the overburden, the base of the sand is the exact mirror of its top, every sample is the same mixture of the two boundaries, and the crossplot collapses to a straight segment through the origin. Clear the underburden checkbox and pick a different rock below the sand: the segment opens into the loop the DQ papers call a torpedo, and the polar angle spread stops reading zero. Neither shape is noise and neither is an error. Both follow from convolving a two-spike reflectivity with a band-limited wavelet, and the torpedo appears in every real AVO crossplot ever made, where it is usually hidden inside a cloud of hundreds of thousands of points.

3Two samples out of sixty

Which of those points is anyone allowed to interpret?

The two-layer equation is cleanest where the waveform is at a peak maximum or a trough minimum. At those two places the sample is dominated by one boundary and the wavelet's own shape is momentarily flat, so reading an amplitude off the trace is close to reading a reflection coefficient. Move away from an extremum and the sample becomes a blend of two boundaries in a proportion that depends on the wavelet and the bed thickness, so an intercept and gradient measured there describe interference as much as rock. The reading does not switch off; it degrades, and it degrades fastest in thin beds.

So a defensible AVO crossplot keeps only the extrema. Here they are marked on the near stack, and here is the crossplot with everything else removed. Keep that count in mind: it is the number the rest of the course is trying to raise, and module 05 is where you see whether it worked.

What is lost when 95% of the trace goes unused

A discarded sample is not a neutral thing. Each one is a place in the reservoir where the seismic data has something to say and the interpretation method has decided not to listen. The losses fall in specific places, and they are the places that matter most.

What goes missing

  • The inside of the reservoir. The peak and trough sit at the top and base of the sand. Every sample between them — the part of the trace that is actually inside the pay — is discarded. Vertical variation in porosity within a sand body is invisible to a method that only looks at its edges.
  • Anything that is not a peak or a trough. A thin bed whose response never develops a clean extremum contributes nothing. Neither does a sand that sits on a zero crossing, which is exactly what happens to a bed below about a quarter of a wavelength.
  • Class 2p sands. When the polarity reverses across the gather, the stacked amplitude passes through zero. The interpretation method most likely to be used on a class 2p sand is the one least equipped to see it.
  • The shale. The samples nearest a strong boundary carry the wavelet's sidelobes, and on a crossplot those boundary shales land right on top of the sand clusters. Filtering them out is module 06; recognizing that they are there at all is this module.

The two ways out

There are broadly two responses to this problem. One is deconvolution and inversion: estimate the wavelet, remove it, and recover something closer to reflectivity or impedance. It works, and it requires a wavelet estimate, well control, and a low-frequency model, none of which exist at the start of a project. It also introduces its own artifacts through spectral whitening.

The other is to convert every sample into the equivalent of a peak or a trough, so that the two-layer equation becomes applicable everywhere without ever estimating a wavelet. That is the DQ trace. It is a strange idea on first hearing, and the next four modules are spent making it ordinary.

Neither route is a replacement for the other. The DQ process does not create events that were not already in the data — it makes the events that are there easier to see and easier to interpret, and it does so early, before the well correlation and wavelet estimation work has been done.

Exercises

  1. One sand, three classes. Set the porosity to 20% and the fluid to oil, and leave the underburden following the overburden. Now step through the three overburden lithologies on step 1, reading the class label each time.
    AnswerSoft shale gives class 1, with an intercept near +0.11. Hard shale gives class 2, intercept near −0.03. The tight sandstone gives class 4, intercept near −0.22. Nothing about the reservoir changed between those three readings. AVO class describes the contrast across a boundary, not the rock underneath it. Where the overburden changes along a single line — a shale passing laterally into a cemented sand, or a compaction boundary crossing the interval — one continuous pay sand will produce several different AVO responses, and an interpreter who fixed the class at the start of the project is wrong over part of the field.
  2. Find the porosity where the sand vanishes. Choose the soft shale overburden and a brine fill, then drag the porosity slider slowly and watch the intercept and the near-stack value.
    AnswerStarting at 24% the label runs class 1, then 2p, then 2, then 3. The intercept passes through zero between 32 and 33%: there the sand and the shale have the same acoustic impedance, the near stack sees nothing at all, and the whole response lives in the gradient. Just below the crossing the intercept is still faintly positive while the far stack has already gone negative, which is what class 2p means — the reflection changes sign partway across the gather, and a full stack cancels much of it. Switch to gas and the same sequence happens about seven porosity units earlier. Neither number means anything on its own; both move the moment the shale does. What is worth keeping is that being invisible on the near stack is a coincidence between two rocks, and it happens to perfectly good reservoirs.
  3. Substitute the fluid and watch three numbers move. Soft shale, porosity 25%. Read the sand's VP, VS and density for brine, then oil, then gas.
    AnswerVP falls from about 3300 to 3130 m/s and density falls from 2.24 to 2.04 g/cm³, both of which you would expect. VS rises, from about 1720 to 1800 m/s. Nothing stiffened: shear velocity is the square root of the shear modulus over the density, the pore fluid has no shear strength and so leaves the modulus alone, and the density went down. The class moves from 1 to 2p on the way. A dim spot and a polarity reversal here are the same sand with different fluid.
  4. Open the torpedo. On step 2, clear the underburden checkbox and put a different rock below the sand, watching the crossplot and the polar angle spread.
    AnswerWhile the underburden follows the overburden the two reflection coefficients are equal and opposite, so the near stack and the far-minus-near difference are the same mixture of the same two wavelets at every sample. One is then a fixed multiple of the other and the crossplot has to be a straight segment through the origin; the polar angle spread reads zero because there is only one angle present. Give the sand a different floor and the base is no longer the mirror of the top, the two axes stop being proportional, and the segment opens into a loop. Real sands are almost never symmetrically encased, which is why the torpedo is what turns up in field data.
  5. Collapse the loop. On step 2, set the thickness to 60 ms and then reduce it to 8 ms, watching the crossplot.
    AnswerAt 60 ms the top and base reflections are separate events and the loop has two clear ends, each near the two-layer answer for one boundary. As the bed thins the two events interfere, the loop shortens, rotates, and pulls away from the crimson cross. By 8 ms — about a third of a period at 40 Hz — nothing on the crossplot is close to the correct two-layer answer, even though the reflection coefficients that generated the trace never changed. The AVO response of a thin bed is a property of the wavelet as much as of the rock.
  6. Change the sample rate and count. On step 3, step the sample rate from 4 ms down to 0.5 ms, reading both counters each time.
    AnswerThe number of samples in the window goes up by a factor of eight. The number AVO can use stays at two per cycle, so the fraction discarded climbs from roughly 80% to roughly 97%. Finer sampling adds numbers, not information, as far as a two-layer method is concerned. The DQ process is the opposite: it converts the extra samples into usable ones, which is why the papers specify a 1 ms sample rate and eight quadrants rather than the 2 ms and four quadrants used earlier.
  7. Lose track of which extremum is the sand. Set the wavelet to 20 Hz and the thickness to 10 ms, then look at the marked trace on step 3 and count the filled circles.
    AnswerThere are four, and only one of them is the top of the sand. The top and base have merged into a single event and the rest are the sidelobes of the wavelet. Nothing on the trace distinguishes them, so an auto-tracker following a loop will happily pick a sidelobe and an AVO analysis will happily interpret it. Thinning the bed does not just reduce the number of usable samples — it makes the surviving ones harder to trust. This is the boundary shale problem that module 06 has to solve.
  8. Check whether the background trend really sits at 45 degrees. Set the fluid to brine and step the porosity across its whole range, watching the point against the dashed B = −A line on the step 1 crossplot.
    AnswerThe brine sands trace out a line of their own, and under the soft shale it is a good deal steeper than B = −A. That 315° background trend comes from assuming VP/VS = 2 in both layers together with Gardner's density relation, and neither assumption holds in the model you just built. Change the overburden and the brine line tilts again. Module 06 returns to this: the RDQ crossplot rotates the display so that whatever the local brine trend actually is lies along the x axis, rather than assuming in advance where it ought to be.

The main points

  • AVO theory describes one boundary between two thick layers. R(θ) = A + B sin²θ is exact for that model and says nothing about bed thickness or wavelets.
  • AVO class is an outcome, not a property of the reservoir. Change only the rock sitting on top and one sand becomes class 1, class 2 and class 4 in turn. That is why a method which needs no prior petrophysics is worth having.
  • Seismic data is many thin layers convolved with a wavelet. Every sample is a blend of several boundaries, in proportions that depend on the wavelet and the bed thickness.
  • The two-layer equation is cleanest at peak maxima and trough minima, and degrades between them. That is two well-conditioned samples per waveform cycle, regardless of how finely the data was recorded.
  • Most of a 1 ms trace is therefore not used quantitatively in a conventional AVO analysis, including most of what lies inside the reservoir.
  • Thin beds lose the most. Below about a quarter of a wavelength the extrema stop corresponding to the top and base of the sand at all.
  • There are two ways to close the gap: estimate and remove the wavelet, or convert every sample into the equivalent of a peak or trough. This course is about the second.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The model is a sand between two half spaces. The enclosing rock is one of three fixed lithologies: soft shale (VP 2700 m/s, VS 1175 m/s, 2.33 g/cm³), hard shale (3350, 1675, 2.48) and tight sandstone (4800, 2595, 2.58). The underburden follows the overburden unless the checkbox is cleared. These three are illustrative rather than measured, chosen to span the range an interpreter meets in one basin.

The sand is not a preset. The brine case is computed from porosity alone, using the two relations in Bedle et al. (2025a): Wyllie's time average for velocity, 1/VP = φ/Vfl + (1 − φ)/Vma with a matrix velocity of 5486 m/s and a brine velocity of 1500 m/s, and the trend VP/VS = −0.75φ² + 1.35φ + 1.63 for shear velocity. Density is the volumetric mixture of quartz at 2.65 g/cm³ with the pore fluid. Porosity is the only control on the brine sand, and the resulting AVO class is read off the computed intercept and gradient rather than declared.

The oil and gas cases are made from the brine case by Gassmann (1951) fluid substitution: the dry-frame bulk modulus is recovered from the brine-saturated moduli, the new fluid is put in, and the velocities are rebuilt from the result. The shear modulus is left unchanged, which is why VS rises slightly when a light fluid replaces brine — the modulus holds while the density drops. Fluid bulk moduli and densities are 2.60 GPa and 1.03 g/cm³ for brine, 0.80 and 0.78 for oil, and 0.055 and 0.20 for gas, with a mineral modulus of 37 GPa. Saturation is treated as complete and uniform; partial and patchy saturation, which matter a great deal in practice, are left out. The AVO basics site works through Gassmann properly.

Intercept and gradient come from the Shuey (1985) two-term approximation, computed from the contrast across each interface rather than assumed. Writing Δ for the difference across the boundary and a bar for the average of the two sides, A = ½(ΔVP/VP + Δρ/ρ) and B = ½ΔVP/VP − 2(VS/VP)²(Δρ/ρ + 2ΔVS/VS). The base-of-sand interface is computed the same way with the two layers swapped, so it is not simply assumed to be the negative of the top.

The near and far stacks are formed by averaging R(θ) over 0–10° and 20–30°, the ranges specified in Bedle et al. (2025a). Because R is linear in sin²θ, the average over a band is A + B times the mean of sin²θ across it, which the page evaluates numerically. Angle-dependent tuning, normal-moveout stretch, transmission loss and mode conversion are all left out. Their absence flatters the conventional method, so the loss demonstrated in step 3 is a lower bound.

Steps 2 and 3 plot the near stack against the raw far-minus-near difference, which is how the DQ workflow forms its gradient, rather than against a calibrated B. Because R is linear in sin²θ, F − N is exactly B times the difference in mean sin²θ across the two bands, a factor of 0.170 for 0–10° and 20–30°. Dividing it out would make the two axes commensurate and the plots easier to read, while showing something the processing never computes, so the module leaves it alone. Every crossplot in the module shares the same fixed axes, ±0.5, and none of them rescale with the model: seeing how much the spread itself changes between one set of rocks and the next is worth more than filling the box each time. Anything past the edge is clipped and counted rather than drawn over the labels.

The traces are the two reflection coefficients convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. Peak maxima and trough minima are found by scanning the near-stack trace for sign changes in the sample to sample difference, which is the same test the DQ quadrant assignment uses in module 03. The polar angle spread quoted in step 2 is the range of atan2(F − N, N) across the samples whose amplitude exceeds a tenth of the largest, which keeps near-zero samples with meaningless angles out of the statistic.

No noise is added anywhere in this module. Everything shown is the consequence of convolution alone.
