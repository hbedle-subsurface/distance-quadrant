Module 01  ·  The DQ trace

Near and far stacks

Everything after this module operates on two traces. They come from one CDP of NMO-corrected gathers by averaging a narrow window of angles twice, at 0–10° and at 20–30°. This module builds those two traces, shows that they are the intercept and the gradient in a thin disguise, and works through why the method takes two averages rather than fitting a line through all the angles at once.

1Two windows on a gather

Where the two input traces come from

The DQ process starts from one CDP of NMO-corrected gathers with relative amplitudes preserved. Every trace in the gather is the same two boundaries seen at a different incidence angle, so the reflection coefficient changes across the gather while the arrival time does not. That is what NMO correction is for, and step 3 shows what happens when it has not quite worked.

Two windows are cut from that gather and the traces inside each are averaged. The near window is 0–10°, the far window is ten degrees wide starting wherever you put it, 20–30° by default. Averaging turns eleven traces into one, and the gather becomes two traces called N and F.

2What the two traces are

The near stack is the intercept, and the difference is the gradient

Module 01 wrote the reflection as R(θ) = A + B sin²θ. Averaging that over an angle window is easy, because R is linear in sin²θ and the average of a straight line over an interval is the line evaluated at the average of the interval. So the near stack is A + B × ⟨sin²θ⟩near and the far stack is A + B × ⟨sin²θ⟩far, with no approximation anywhere.

Put the numbers in. Over 0–10° the mean of sin²θ is 0.0101, and over 20–30° it is 0.1802. That makes the near stack the intercept plus a hundredth of the gradient, which is why the DQ papers write N and mean A. Subtract the two and the intercept cancels exactly, leaving the gradient multiplied by the difference of those two numbers, 0.1701.

3Why not fit a line through all of it?

The honest comparison, including the part that favors the regression

An obvious objection: the gather has forty-one traces and the two windows use twenty-two of them. Why not fit R = A + B sin²θ through every angle at every sample and get A and B directly? That is a real method, it is what AVO inversion does, and on clean data it is the better estimator. Fitting all forty-one angles measures the gradient with about four times less variance than taking the difference of two window averages. There is no argument to be had about that.

The reasons to take two averages instead are about what real gathers do. The readouts below separate the two things that go wrong. Noise is the standard error each estimator carries, which follows from the geometry and needs no particular realization to work out. Bias is measured on a noiseless gather, so it is purely what the leftover moveout did. Turn the noise up first, then leave it alone and drag the moveout.

They do not win the same one. With four milliseconds of moveout at 40° the two-stack gradient is out by 30% and the fitted gradient by 71%. The intercept is starker: the near stack is still exact, while the fitted intercept is out by 23%, and by eight milliseconds it is out by half. The fit is the more precise estimator and the more biased one.

A window ten degrees wide spans a small fraction of the moveout across the gather, so the traces inside it are still aligned with each other when they are averaged, and the near window spans almost none of it at all. A fit across the whole gather compares the same sample index at 0° and at 40°, which after a few milliseconds of leftover curvature are different parts of the waveform. The fit does not know that. It returns a confident number with a small standard error and the wrong value in it.

?Why the method wants traces, not coefficients

The reason that has nothing to do with statistics

The strongest reason the DQ process takes partial stacks only becomes visible once you know what happens next. Everything from module 02 onward reads structure off the near stack: where it crosses zero, where its peaks are, where its troughs are. Those picks define the acoustic layers, and every quadrant number and phase filter in the method hangs off them. So the question that matters is not which estimator has the smaller error bar. It is which one gives you a near-angle trace you can trust the shape of.

On that question step 3 is one-sided. The near stack averages ten degrees of angle, over which the leftover moveout is a fraction of a millisecond, and its intercept stays exact however far you push the moveout slider. The fitted intercept has to extrapolate back to zero angle from a lever arm forty degrees long, and by eight milliseconds it is out by half. A trace that is wrong by half is not something to pick zero crossings on.

The rest of the practical case

  • Partial stacks are what exists. Near and far volumes are a standard deliverable. Full-fold NMO-corrected gathers with relative amplitude preserved are often not handed to the interpreter at all, and when they are, they arrive late.
  • Two traces can be scaled and checked. The papers call for time-variant scaling on the far trace so that its peak amplitudes match what AVO theory predicts. That is a tractable job on one trace pair and an awkward one on a coefficient volume.
  • The two-term equation has a range. Shuey's approximation is written for small angles and starts to drift somewhere past 30°. A window that stays inside the valid range is honest about that; a fit that runs to 40° and beyond quietly folds the error into the gradient.
  • Narrow windows survive imperfect processing. Residual moveout, normal-moveout stretch and angle-dependent tuning all get worse with offset. Averaging ten degrees of them is a much smaller crime than regressing across forty.
None of this makes AVO inversion wrong. It is a different tool with different inputs and a different place in the sequence. The DQ trace is meant to run early, on what is available, before the well ties and the wavelet estimation, and the two partial stacks are the earliest usable thing there is.

Exercises

  1. Check that the near stack really is the intercept. On step 2, read the largest gap between N and A, then compare it with the intercept itself for a few models.
    AnswerThe gap is A + 0.0101 B minus A, which is a hundredth of the gradient. Across the whole model it never exceeds about 0.007, and for most sands it is nearer 0.002. Set against intercepts of a tenth to half, that is a rounding error, and it is why the DQ papers use the near stack as the intercept without further comment. It is not an approximation to the physics — the physics is exact — it is a decision that one hundredth of B is not worth carrying around.
  2. Move the far window and watch the gradient scale. On step 2, drag the far window from 12° out to 45°, reading the multiplier on F − N.
    AnswerIt climbs steeply: 0.0775 at a 12–22° window, 0.1701 at 20–30°, 0.3198 at 30–40°. Moving the window out by eighteen degrees roughly quadruples it. A wider separation gives the gradient more leverage and a larger number to work with, which is why acquisition geometry matters so much for AVO. The catch is everything in the Why it matters tab: the far traces are the noisiest, they carry the most moveout error and the most stretch, and past about 30° the two-term equation is no longer describing the same physics. 20–30° is a compromise, not a law.
  3. Be fair to the fit first. On step 3, leave the residual moveout at zero and raise the noise to 40%, then read the two gradient noise figures.
    Answer57% of the true gradient for the two stacks against 28% for the fit — almost exactly half, which is the square root of the factor of four in variance. The fit uses all forty-one traces; the difference of two window averages uses eleven and eleven, and then divides by 0.1701, which magnifies whatever noise survived. On a flat gather the fit is simply the better estimator, and if that were the whole story this module would end differently.
  4. Now break it with four milliseconds. Set the noise back to zero and raise the residual moveout to 4 ms, reading the two gradient bias figures.
    Answer30% for the two stacks against 71% for the fit. The near window spans about a third of a millisecond of the moveout and the far window about a millisecond, so the traces inside each are still lined up when they are averaged. The fit compares the same sample index at 0° and at 40°, four milliseconds apart on the waveform, and reports the difference between two parts of a wavelet as a property of the rock. Four milliseconds is not a badly processed gather. It is an ordinary one.
  5. Watch the intercept while you do it. Push the moveout on to 8 ms and read the two intercept bias figures.
    AnswerThe near stack is still exact. The fitted intercept is out by about half. The near stack averages ten degrees of angle, over which the moveout is a fraction of a millisecond, and it never has to extrapolate anywhere. The fit reaches back to zero angle along a forty degree lever arm, and every millisecond of curvature along that arm tilts the answer. This is the one that decides the argument: module 02 picks zero crossings, peaks and troughs off the near stack, and module 03 writes quadrant numbers into the gaps between them. Those picks have to be in the right place, and precision on the gradient is no consolation if they are not.
  6. Find a model where the far stack is the only thing that sees the sand. Try the soft shale with gas, around 25% porosity, and read the two key numbers on step 1.
    AnswerThe near stack sits close to zero while F − N is clearly negative, and the far stack has the opposite sign to the near. This is the class 2p case from module 01, seen through the two traces the method actually works on. A full stack would average the two and lose most of it. The DQ process never forms a full stack, which is one of the quieter advantages of keeping the near and far apart.

The main points

  • The DQ process runs on two traces. A near stack over 0–10° and a far stack over 20–30°, from one CDP of NMO-corrected, relative-amplitude gathers.
  • Averaging a window is exact, not approximate. R is linear in sin²θ, so the average over a window is A + B times the mean of sin²θ across it.
  • N = A + 0.0101 B. The near stack is the intercept to within a hundredth of the gradient, which across the whole model is never more than about 0.007.
  • F − N = 0.1701 B. The intercept cancels exactly and what is left is the gradient scaled by the separation between the two windows. The scale factor is not divided out, so gradients always plot small next to intercepts.
  • On a flat gather a least-squares fit is the better estimator, carrying about half the noise on the gradient. The case for partial stacks is robustness and availability, not precision.
  • Residual moveout biases the fit about twice as hard. At 4 ms the two-stack gradient is out by 30% and the fitted gradient by 71%, because a ten degree window spans a fraction of the moveout and a forty degree fit spans all of it.
  • The near stack's intercept is immune to moveout and the fitted one is not. At 8 ms the near stack is still exact while the fit is out by half, because the fit has to extrapolate to zero angle along a forty degree lever arm.
  • That is what decides it. Module 02 picks zero crossings, peaks and troughs off the near stack, and every quadrant number afterwards depends on those picks landing in the right place.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The rocks are the same as module 01 and are computed the same way: three fixed enclosing lithologies, a brine sand built from porosity through Wyllie's time average and the VP/VS trend of Bedle et al. (2025a), and Gassmann fluid substitution for the oil and gas cases. The underburden follows the overburden throughout this module. Intercept and gradient come from the Shuey (1985) two-term approximation across each interface.

The gather is forty-one traces at one degree spacing from 0 to 40°. Each is the two reflection coefficients evaluated at that angle and convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet, sampled at 1 ms. Window averages are unweighted means of the traces whose angle falls inside the window, which is what a partial stack is. Because R is linear in sin²θ, the average over a window equals A + B times the mean of sin²θ across it; the page evaluates that mean numerically rather than taking the value at the window center, and the difference between the two matters at the third decimal.

The least-squares comparison in step 3 fits R = A + B sin²θ independently at every time sample, using all forty-one traces, by ordinary least squares on sin²θ. Its variance advantage over the two-window difference follows from the spread of sin²θ across the gather against the eleven traces in each window, and works out at about a factor of four in the gradient for this geometry.

Residual moveout is modeled as a time shift proportional to sin²θ, quoted in milliseconds at 40°. Real leftover moveout is not that tidy, but the point of the step is the ratio between what a narrow window averages and what a full-gather fit averages, and that ratio is set by the geometry rather than by the shape of the residual. Normal-moveout stretch, angle dependent tuning, transmission loss and mode conversion are all left out. Every one of them would make the fit look worse than it does here.

Noise is band-limited with the same wavelet, drawn independently for each trace in the gather, and scaled as a percentage of the strongest reflection coefficient in the model. It is regenerated only when the model changes, so dragging a slider does not make the noise dance.

The real workflow adds two steps this module leaves out. Both stacks are bandpass filtered if the signal to noise demands it, since the method cares more about a clean trace than a high peak frequency. The far trace is then given a time-variant scalar so that its peak amplitudes agree with what AVO theory predicts, before anything else is computed. Both traces are sync interpolated to 1 ms and treated as integer samples from that point on.
