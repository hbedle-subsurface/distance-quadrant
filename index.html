How the DQ trace actually works
Modules About AASPI

AASPI  ·  University of Oklahoma

AVO theory is cleanest at two samples per cycle. The DQ trace extends it to the rest.

The equations behind amplitude variation with offset describe a single boundary between two thick layers. Real seismic data is many thin layers convolved with a wavelet, so a sample generally carries the response of more than one boundary at once. The two-layer reading is cleanest at a peak maximum or a trough minimum, where that mixing is least, and it degrades steadily between them. Through most of a reservoir interior, an intercept and gradient measured sample by sample are describing interference as much as rock.

The distance and quadrant trace converts every sample into the equivalent of a peak or a trough, so the two-layer equation becomes usable everywhere, without estimating a wavelet and without well control. These modules build that idea one step at a time, with nothing to install.

What you should be able to do afterwards. Explain what the DQ distance and &thetas;px each measure and what controls them; say why the workflow picks, numbers and phase-filters a trace before combining the stacks; read a DQ section and know which features are rock and which are bed thickness; and recognise where the attributes become unreliable.

What you need first. You should know what a seismic trace is, what a reflector is, and roughly what AVO is trying to do. If intercept, gradient and crossplots are new, work through How AVO Actually Works first. Everything after that is built up in order here.

Most of a trace goes unused

At 1 ms sampling and 40 Hz, a cycle is about 25 samples and only two of them sit at an extremum. The rest are recorded and displayed, and are rarely interpreted quantitatively.

No wavelet, no wells, no mudrock line

The DQ process needs a near stack and a far stack and nothing else. That is what lets AVO work start at the beginning of a project rather than the end.

It does not invent events

Every feature on a DQ section is already in the near and far stacks. The process makes them easier to see and easier to quantify, and you can always go back and check.

About

The distance and quadrant trace was developed at Phillips Petroleum and has been carried forward by the AASPI consortium at the University of Oklahoma. These modules are a teaching companion to two papers in Interpretation: Bedle et al. (2024) on the DQ trace itself, and Bedle et al. (2025) on the RDQ crossplot workflow.

Everything here runs in the browser. There is no server, no upload and no install, and no data you generate leaves the page.
