How the DQ trace actually works
Modules About AASPI

AASPI  ·  University of Oklahoma

AVO theory is cleanest at two samples per cycle. The DQ trace is designed to use the rest.

The equations behind amplitude variation with offset (AVO) describe a single boundary between two thick layers. Seismic data record many layers, each reflection spread out by the wavelet, so one sample generally carries the response of more than one boundary. The two-layer reading is cleanest at a peak or a trough, where one boundary dominates, and it becomes less reliable between them.

The distance and quadrant (DQ) workflow, developed by Dennis Neff and Warren Neff at Phillips Petroleum and published with the AASPI group (Bedle et al., 2025a, 2025b, 2025c), converts each sample of a near and a far partial stack to the equivalent of a peak or trough. The two-layer reading can then be applied down the whole trace, before a wavelet is estimated or a well is tied. The modules build the workflow one operation at a time and show where it works less well.

Who it is for

Undergraduate geology and geophysics students meeting these methods for the first time. The modules assume a working idea of a seismic trace, a reflection and what AVO sets out to measure. Students new to intercept, gradient and crossplots may find How AVO Actually Works a useful first step.

What the workflow needs

A near and a far partial stack, prepared with zero phase and relative amplitudes. No wavelet estimate, well tie or mudrock-line slope is needed, so AVO work can begin early in a project, and each feature on a DQ section can be checked against the stacks it came from (Bedle et al., 2025a).

After the modules

A student should be able to explain what the DQ and Theta PX measure and what controls them; describe why the workflow picks, numbers and phase-rotates the near stack before combining it with the far stack; recognize on a DQ section which features can come from the rock and which from bed thickness; and name the conditions under which the attributes become unreliable.

About

The distance and quadrant trace was developed by Dennis B. Neff and Warren Neff at Phillips Petroleum (Neff, 2003; Neff et al., 2005) and has been published with the AASPI consortium at the University of Oklahoma. These modules are a teaching companion to three papers in Interpretation: Bedle et al. (2025a) on the DQ trace, Bedle et al. (2025b) on the RDQ crossplot workflow, and Bedle et al. (2025c) on RDQ inversion. The signed isochron attributes follow Choudhry et al. (2026).
